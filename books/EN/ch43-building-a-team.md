# Building and Leading a Team of Buzzer-Ringers

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Recruit for the traits that predict success at the buzzer, not for a polished sales CV.
- Separate training (teach the method once) from coaching (fix the gaps forever).
- Design incentives and quotas that drive the right behaviour, not just the number.
- Equip a team with sales enablement so a new hire produces senior-quality output in week one.
- Scale one good seller into many without losing what made the first one work.

## Key concepts

Everything so far has been about one person at one intercom. A team is the same act,
repeated by many people at once. That sounds simple and is not. The method that made
you good does not transfer by osmosis. If you hire ten people and hand them a product
sheet, you get ten strangers improvising at ten buzzers. If you hire for the right
traits, teach one method, coach the gaps, and give them the tools, you get one
machine with ten hands. This chapter is about building that machine.

### Recruiting for the buzzer

Hire for traits, not for a résumé. A CV tells you where someone has been; it does
not tell you what they do when the intercom says "not interested." The traits that
predict success at the buzzer:

- **Resilience to rejection.** The person who rings the next door after a hard no,
  not the person who needs a week to recover.
- **Coachability.** They can hear a correction without defending, and change the
  next time. This is the single best predictor of growth.
- **Curiosity.** They want to know the customer's problem, not just deliver a pitch.
- **Hunger with patience.** They chase the goal daily but do not force a deal that
  is not there.
- **Integrity.** They keep their word when no one is watching and no commission is
  on the line.

Interview for behaviour, not for charm. Ask for a real story: "Tell me about a time
a customer told you no to your face. What did you do next?" A smooth talker who has
never lost is a risk; a seller who lost, learned, and went back is a hire. Check
references for coachability specifically — ask the last manager, "How did they take
feedback?" A team is a chain. One bad hire poisons the territory, the morale, and
the referrals of everyone beside them.

### Training and coaching

These are two different jobs. Most managers confuse them and do neither well.

**Training** is transferring the method once. The 30-second pitch. The discovery
questions. The objection answers. The close for the next step. It is group work,
front-loaded, before the rep ever rings a buzzer alone. A new hire should leave
training able to run the whole conversation, even if clumsily.

**Coaching** is ongoing, individual, and after the live work. You listen to them
ring the buzzer — a ride-along, a recorded call, a role-play — and you fix one
thing at a time. Not five things. One. Then you watch the next attempt. Coaching is
not telling someone they were wrong; it is showing them the one move that works
better and letting them practise it until it is theirs.

A manager who only coaches, without training first, is guessing at a foundation
that was never laid. A manager who only trains, without coaching after, lets the
skill decay within a month. Do both: train for a week, coach forever.

### Incentives and quotas

A **quota** is the number a rep must hit. A **commission** (or incentive) is the
money tied to it. Together they are the loudest signal you send about what you
actually want. Design them with three rules:

- **Pay on the behaviour you want.** If you want new customers, reward new-logo
  bookings, not just renewals. If you reward only revenue, you get sellers who farm
  the easy accounts and never hunt.
- **Make it computable in one line.** A rep who cannot work out their own commission
  in their head will not chase it. "Ten per cent of every signed deal, plus a bonus
  for every new logo" is clear. A five-tab spreadsheet is not.
- **Avoid cliff-edges and traps.** A plan with a hard threshold ("nothing below 80
  per cent") pushes reps to sandbag — hold deals back to land next quarter — or to
  game the number instead of serving the customer. Smooth the curve.

Balance the individual and the team. Pure individual plans make heroes and hoarders;
pure team plans hide free-riders. A common mix: individual commission on personal
bookings, plus a small team bonus so the group helps each other. And recognise what
money cannot buy: name the best "no" of the week — the hardest rejection handled
well. You get what you celebrate.

### Sales enablement

**Sales enablement** is everything that helps a rep sell without you standing
there: the pitch deck, the objection library, the proposal templates, the CRM, the
prospect research, the training material. The goal is one sentence: a new rep can
produce senior-quality output in their first week.

Enablement kills the two excuses that lose deals — "I don't know how" and "I don't
have the material." When the deck, the proposal, and the research are one ask away,
the rep spends their energy on the person at the buzzer instead of on the paperwork.
Keep it in one place, current, and searchable. Stale enablement is worse than none:
it teaches the wrong answer with confidence.

### Scaling the method

Scaling means turning one good seller into many. The trap is that the best seller's
method lives in their head and never leaves. The fix is to **codify** it: write down
the steps, the questions, the answers, the order. Then a new rep follows the
codified method, not a poor copy of the star. The star is no longer the bottleneck.

Scale in **cohorts** — a class of new hires trained together. They bond, they share
what they learn, they set a common standard, and you train once instead of one by
one. Then grow only as fast as you can coach. A team you cannot coach decays: the
method drifts, the new hires copy each other's mistakes, and quality falls below the
level that made the buzzer work. Add a manager for every handful of reps, and a
coaching cadence that does not slip when the quarter gets busy.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Recruiting | Choosing who joins the team | "Recruit for coachability, not charm." |
| Quota | The number a rep must hit | "His quota is forty new logos a year." |
| Commission | Money tied to the sale | "Her commission is ten per cent of each deal." |
| Sales enablement | Tools and material that help the rep sell | "Enablement let the new hire send a senior-level proposal on day three." |
| Coaching | Ongoing, one-on-one fixing of the live work | "Coaching one move per week changed his close rate." |
| Cohort | A group trained together | "The new cohort trained for a week and shared every lesson." |
| Sandbagging | Holding deals back to land them later | "A cliff-edge quota invites sandbagging." |
| Ride-along | Watching a rep work live | "On the ride-along I heard the same missed question twice." |

> Every Friday at 5pm, summarise this week's sales and list what is due next week.

**Sales angle:** a manager's leverage is consistent output from many people. With
the training deck made on demand and the weekly report scheduled, the team always
has the material and always sees the numbers. You stop being the person who chases
spreadsheets and start being the person who coaches — which is the only part of
the job that actually compounds.

## A field note

Most sales teams are trained on folklore — what the loudest manager believes. In
the 1980s a research firm called Huthwaite did the opposite. Over a large study it
observed **35,000 sales calls** made by **10,000 salespeople** to find what
actually predicted winning, and published the result as **SPIN Selling** by Neil
Rackham in 1988. The finding that upended the industry: in large, complex sales,
traditional closing pressure did not predict success — what predicted success was
how well the seller asked questions to uncover the buyer's problem (the four types:
Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff). The lesson for a team builder is
direct: train your people on what the evidence says works, not on what feels
forceful. A method you can point to beats a method you have to believe in. (Source:
Neil Rackham, *SPIN Selling*, 1988; Huthwaite Inc. research, as reported by
Close.io.)

## Exercises

1. **Codify your method.** Write your own best sale as six numbered steps: how you
   open, what you ask, how you handle the main objection, how you close for the next
   step. If you cannot write it, you cannot teach it. Revise until a stranger could
   follow it.
2. **Design a one-week training program.** Lay out five days for a new hire: what
   they learn each morning, what they practise each afternoon, and what they must
   be able to do by Friday before ringing a buzzer alone.
3. **Build the one-line plan.** Write an incentive plan a rep can compute in their
   head in one sentence. Then check it against the traps: does it reward the
   behaviour you want? Does it invite sandbagging or gaming? Fix it until both
   answers are right.
4. **Set the coaching cadence.** Write the structure of your weekly one-on-one:
   what you review, the one question you ask ("what was hardest this week?"), and
   the one move you will coach. Put it in the calendar so it does not slip.

## Summary

- Recruit for resilience, coachability, curiosity and integrity — behaviour, not charm.
- Train the method once, coach the gaps forever; one correction at a time.
- Design incentives that pay the behaviour you want and can be computed in one line.
- Sales enablement removes "I don't know how" so a new hire ships senior work in week one.
- Scale by codifying the method, hiring in cohorts, and never growing faster than you can coach — and let AgentBridge make the training decks and the weekly report so the manager can coach instead of chase paperwork.
