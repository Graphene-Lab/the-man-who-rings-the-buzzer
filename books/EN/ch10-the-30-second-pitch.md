# The 30-Second Opening Pitch

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Deliver a 30-second opening with a clear five-part shape.
- Personalise the pitch for one company and one role before you walk in.
- Turn a monologue into a dialogue with two or three qualification questions.
- Adjust the pitch for American, German and Italian business cultures.
- Build three working versions of your own pitch and time them.

## Key concepts

The buzzer (the intercom at the door) buys you attention. The opening pitch decides
what happens with it. You have roughly thirty seconds before the person in front of
you decides, quietly, whether you are worth another minute. A pitch is not a
script you memorise and repeat. It is a shape you fill differently for every door.
Get the shape right and thirty seconds is plenty.

### Why thirty seconds

Attention at a door is short and generous at the same time. People give you half a
minute because it costs them nothing. They spend that half minute asking one
question in their head: "Is this about me, or about you?" A pitch that talks about
your company for thirty seconds fails that test. A pitch that names their problem
in ten passes it. The limit is not a trick. It is a discipline that forces you to
lead with them.

### The five-part shape

A working opening pitch has five parts. Each part is one or two sentences.

| Part | Job | Length | Example line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | Stop the mind from closing the door | 5 sec | "Every bag of coffee you sell carries a story — most are printed badly." |
| Problem | Name their pain in their words | 5–8 sec | "Packaging delays leave shelves half-empty and customers impatient." |
| Solution | What you do, tied to that pain | 5 sec | "We supply roasters with printed packaging on a standing order, delivered in two days." |
| Proof | One concrete fact: a name, a number, a result | 5–7 sec | "We run this for three roasters in the region; none has missed a shelf since March." |
| Ask | One small next step | 3–5 sec | "Could I leave a sample and book fifteen minutes with whoever handles purchasing?" |

Notice the order. The hook is a question or an image, never "My name is and my
company does." The problem belongs to them. The proof is one thing, not five —
one number, one named client, one visible result. The ask is small, because a
small ask gets a yes and a big ask gets a "send me an email."

### Personalising by company and role

The shape stays. The words change. Before any door, change two parts: the problem
line and the ask. Personalise the problem with one specific fact about the company
— a product, a shop, a delay you saw yourself. "I visited your second location on
Tuesday and noticed the corner shelf was empty by four o'clock" beats any generic
line, because it proves you did your homework.

Then match the ask to the role in front of you.

| Role | What they care about | Their ask |
|---|---|---|
| Owner / CEO | Money, growth, risk | "Fifteen minutes on what this saves per month." |
| Operations manager | Time, errors, smooth days | "A quick look at how the standing order removes a weekly headache." |
| Purchasing | Cost, reliability, paper | "Leave the price list and a sample for the file." |
| Technician / user | Ease, fewer complaints | "Show me the thing you use it on, for five minutes." |

Same product, same solution line. Different problem, different ask. That is the
whole of personalisation.

### Rapid qualification questions

A pitch should not end with your last word. It should end inside a question. A
qualification question is a question that checks fit while you are still talking —
so you learn whether to invest in this door. Three that work inside the opening:

- "Am I right that you handle this yourself here?" — checks the role.
- "Is this a priority this year, or am I early?" — checks timing.
- "How are you dealing with it today?" — checks the current state and opens the
  next conversation.

These questions turn the pitch from a broadcast into a conversation. If the
answer is "not a priority," you have learned it in thirty seconds instead of
thirty follow-up emails.

### Variants

You will pitch in different rooms. Keep one core and cut for the venue. The phone
variant drops the physical proof and ends with a calendar slot. The trade-show
variant is louder, shorter, and ends by handing over a card with a question on
it. The dinner variant is slower and mostly questions. Same five parts, different
volume and different ask.

### Cultural adaptation

The same pitch lands differently by culture. These are tendencies, not rules —
always read the person in front of you.

| Culture | What it tends to reward | How to adjust |
|---|---|---|
| USA | Speed, benefit, confidence | Lead with the payoff, keep energy up, ask for the next step directly. |
| Germany | Precision, facts, formality | Use titles (Herr/Frau plus surname), give numbers, avoid hype, allow a longer decision runway. |
| Italy | Relationship, warmth, trust in the person | Greet formally (the polite "Lei"), spend a minute on the person and the firm before business, pitch softly, play the long game. |

In the USA a slow opening loses the room. In Germany a big claim without a number
loses trust. In Italy a pitch that skips the human greeting feels cold and gets
shown the door. The shape survives all three; the speed, the proof and the first
minute of warmth change.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Elevator pitch | A summary you can deliver in an elevator ride | "Her elevator pitch got the CEO to stop and listen." |
| Hook | The first line that stops the mind | "His hook was a question about their empty shelves." |
| Ask | The one next step you request | "My ask was fifteen minutes, not a contract." |
| Qualification question | A question that checks fit early | "'Is this a priority this year?' saved me three months." |
| Variant | A version of the pitch for one situation | "The trade-show variant is shorter and ends with a card." |

## A field note

The "elevator pitch" has a documented ancestor. In his 1972 book *The Art of
Getting Your Own Sweet Way*, quality-management pioneer Philip B. Crosby told how,
as Director of Quality at ITT, he planned a speech about a change he wanted and
waited at the elevator in headquarters. He stepped on with the CEO and delivered
the prepared speech. By the time the doors reached the CEO's floor, Crosby had
been asked to present the idea to a meeting of all the general managers. The
modern 30-second pitch is his trick, renamed. (Source: Wikipedia, "Elevator
pitch," citing ASQ *Quality Progress*, May 2011.)

## Exercises

1. **The five lines.** Write your hook, problem, solution, proof and ask for your
   real offer — one line each. Read it aloud. It must fit in thirty seconds.
2. **Three versions.** Rewrite the pitch for three roles at one target company:
   the owner, the operations manager, the buyer. Change the problem line and the
   ask; keep the solution. You now have three doors covered with one shape.
3. **The stopwatch drill.** Pitch to a partner with a stopwatch. Cut twenty per
   cent of the words and repeat until you land at twenty-eight seconds without
   rushing.
4. **Cultural swap.** Deliver the same pitch three ways: American, German,
   Italian. Notice what you change — speed, proof, warmth — and say why.

## Summary

- A pitch is a shape, not a script: hook, problem, solution, proof, ask.
- Personalise the problem and the ask with one real fact; keep the solution
  stable.
- End inside a qualification question so the pitch becomes a conversation.
- Culture changes the delivery: speed in the USA, proof in Germany, warmth in
  Italy.
- AgentBridge rebuilds the deck per client so personalisation keeps pace with
  your day.
