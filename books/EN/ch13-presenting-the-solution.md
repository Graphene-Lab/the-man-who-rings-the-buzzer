# Presenting the Solution: From Product to Value

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Turn every feature of your offer into a benefit this specific customer cares about.
- Run a value-based presentation: sell the outcome, not the product.
- Write a personalised value statement for a named prospect.
- Use social proof and case studies without overclaiming.
- Use ROI and TCO numbers in a presentation and make them believable.

## Key concepts

Discovery gave you the customer's own words. The presentation is where you hand
those words back to them, attached to your solution. The amateur presents the
product: here is what it is, here is what it costs. The professional presents
the value: here is what your week looks like after this change. The buzzer got
you the meeting. The presentation decides whether the meeting meant anything.

### Features, benefits, and the bridge

A **feature** is what the thing is or does. A **benefit** is what that means for
this customer. The two are not the same sentence, and most sellers never cross
the gap between them. The crossing tool is one phrase: **"which means that."**

| Feature | Bridge | Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| The system checks stock every night | which means that | you never promise a customer an item you do not have |
| The report cites its sources | which means that | your buyer can check every claim in five minutes |
| The machine runs eight hours on one charge | which means that | your couriers finish the route without hunting for a socket |

Run the bridge three times if you must. The first stop is usually still a
feature dressed up. Keep going until you land on something the customer would
pay for — time saved, money saved, risk removed, status gained. And check the
anchor: a benefit that does not trace back to a need they stated in discovery is
noise, however clever it sounds.

### Value-Based Selling

**Value-based selling** means selling the value, not the product. Value is the
customer's own judgement: what they gain against what they pay. It is not a
spec sheet and it is not a discount. Price is one number. Value is many —
hours, errors, stress, growth, reputation. The rule that follows is simple and
breaks most presentations: **never present the price before the value.** If
your first number is the price, you have invited a price comparison. If your
first numbers are theirs — their hours, their losses, their growth — the
comparison is on ground you chose.

### The value statement

A **value statement** is two or three personalised sentences that carry the
whole sale. Its shape:

> their problem, in their words + your solution + a quantified outcome + a
> proof point.

For a café owner who told you she loses two hours a day to stock counting:

> "You said counting stock eats two hours every morning. AgentBridge counts it
> for you overnight, which gives you back ten hours a week — and Bright Cafe
> has run it since March without a single stock-out."

For a logistics manager worried about late promises:

> "Late delivery quotes cost you two customers last quarter. Our system checks
> live stock before the quote goes out, so every date you promise is a date you
> can keep — the same way it holds for our three other transport clients."

Say it aloud. Cut every word that does no work. If the statement could belong
to any customer, it belongs to none — rewrite it until it sounds like their
meeting, not your brochure.

### Social proof and case studies

Customers do not trust what you say about yourself. They trust what people
like them have already done. **Social proof** is that trust borrowed: a named
testimonial, a customer count, a logo, a peer reference, a case study.

A case study has three beats. **Before:** the pain, with a number. "Nine hours
a week on manual invoicing." **Change:** what was done, briefly. "Invoices
generated from the job list, sent the same day." **Result:** the number after,
and a quote. "Invoicing now takes forty minutes. Their words: 'I stopped
dreading Fridays.'"

Two honesty rules. First, never invent a case and never round a number upward —
one checkable lie ends every future check. Second, match the case to the
persona: the owner hears the owner's story, the finance director hears the
finance director's. One real case per persona, kept current, beats ten vague
claims.

### ROI and TCO

Two numbers finish the argument in your favour when the buyer reaches for the
wallet.

**ROI (return on investment)**: what you gain, minus what you pay, divided by
what you pay, as a percentage. **Payback period**: how long until the gains
cover the cost. **TCO (total cost of ownership)**: the whole price of
possessing something — purchase price plus running costs over its life.

Worked example. A system costs 4,800 euros a year. It saves six hours a week
of staff time at 30 euros an hour: 180 euros a week, about 9,360 euros a
year. Gain minus cost is 4,560 euros. ROI is roughly 95 percent, and the
system pays for itself in about six months.

| | Printer A | Printer B |
|---|---|---|
| Sticker price | 300 | 550 |
| Running cost per page | 0.06 | 0.03 |
| Pages per year | 20,000 | 20,000 |
| Three-year TCO | 3,900 | 2,350 |

The cheap printer is the expensive one. Present TCO and the "expensive"
option often becomes the saving.

Three rules for numbers. Be conservative: use the low end of your savings
estimate, so the prospect can underwrite it themselves. Show the arithmetic:
a number with its working visible survives a finance director; a bare number
does not. Let them do the last step: "If I am wrong on the savings and you
save half, you still break even by spring" is more convincing than any
guarantee.

### The shape of the presentation

Five moves, twenty minutes, conversation left over. One: restate their
situation in their words — proof you listened. Two: the value statement.
Three: proof — the case study, the comparison, the live demo. Four: the
numbers — ROI, payback, TCO, shown with the working. Five: the next step,
which is the appointment (the closing chapter takes it from there). Rehearse
the first two moves until they are automatic; a strong opening carries the
rest.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Feature | What the product is or does | "Fast charging is a feature." |
| Benefit | What the feature means for this customer | "Finishing the route on one charge is the benefit." |
| Value-based selling | Selling the gain, not the goods | "She never quotes the price before the value." |
| Value statement | A short, personalised claim of value | "His value statement used her own words back to her." |
| Social proof | Trust borrowed from other customers | "Three named cafés were the social proof that closed it." |
| Case study | Before, change, result — with numbers | "The case study opened with nine wasted hours." |
| ROI | Gain minus cost, over cost, as a percent | "Ninety-five percent ROI made the debate short." |
| Payback period | Time until gains cover the cost | "A six-month payback needs no committee." |
| TCO | The whole cost of owning, over its life | "On TCO, the dear printer was cheaper." |

## AgentBridge in action

A value presentation is only as strong as its proof. Hand the prospect a
document that proves the market before you ever open the product:

> Produce a PDF market analysis of the specialty coffee retail segment: size,
> trends, competitors and outlook.

The agent researches the topic and writes a structured PDF report with
sections, figures and cited sources — the kind of document that normally
takes a consultant days. Ask for "short brief" or "detailed report" to set
the depth. You open the meeting with "here is your market and where the gap
is," not "here is my product." The cited sources make every claim checkable,
and checkable is believable. The same pattern covers the money slide: "Create
a PDF financial summary for 2025: revenue, costs, profit and a
year-over-year chart."

**Sales angle:** value you can hand across the table — a cited report does the
proving while you do the asking.

## A field note

Value selling has an academic grandfather. In 1959 the Harvard Business
School professor Theodore Levitt published "Marketing Myopia" in the Harvard
Business Review, built on one question: "What business are you in?" His point
was that companies go blind when they fall in love with their product instead
of the customer's need — a railroad that thinks it is in the railroad
business, not the transportation business. In *The Marketing Imagination*
(1983) he put the alternative plainly: the purpose of a business is "rather
than merely making money... to create and keep a customer." Every value-based
presentation since is that sentence turned into a meeting. (Source: Wikipedia,
"Theodore Levitt.")

## Exercises

1. **Bridge to the bone.** Take your five main features. Write three
   "which means that" links for each. Stop only when you reach time, money,
   risk or status — and check that the customer named that need in discovery.
2. **Three prospects, three statements.** Pick three real prospects with
   different personas. Write a value statement for each, using their own
   words from your last conversation. Read them aloud; cut every word that
   does no work. Swap with a partner: can they tell which customer each one is
   for?
3. **One honest case.** Build one before/change/result case study from a real
   customer, with real numbers and written permission. Present it in ninety
   seconds.
4. **The five-minute number.** Put your ROI, payback and TCO on one page with
   the arithmetic visible. Present it in under five minutes to someone who
   is allowed to challenge every figure.

## Summary

- Features are facts; benefits are consequences; "which means that" is the
  bridge between them.
- Sell the value, not the product: never let the price be your first number.
- A value statement is personalised — their pain, your fix, a number, a
  proof — and unrecognisable as generic copy.
- Real, checkable proof beats loud claims: one case study per persona.
- ROI, payback and TCO turn belief into arithmetic; keep them conservative
  and show the working.
