# Territory Management: Where, When, and How to Organise Visits

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Treat geography as a strategic variable, not just a map of where you live.
- Zone a territory and route a day to maximise buzzers per kilometre.
- Read the temporal patterns of when companies are most receptive.
- Plan a week and a day so driving time is small and ringing time is large.
- Apply the "buzzer density" principle and use maps, CRM, and routing tools to design a territory.

## Key concepts

A buzzer costs money in two currencies: courage and kilometres. The beginner spends
both badly, crossing town for one meeting and burning the day on a motorway. The
professional treats the map as a weapon. Territory management is the discipline of
deciding where you go, when you go, and in what order, so that every hour on the
road returns the most buzzers.

### Geography as a strategic variable

Distance is not neutral. Every kilometre you drive is a minute you are not ringing.
A seller who scatters visits across a whole region spends most of the day in the
car and a little of it at doors. A seller who concentrates visits in a small area
spends a little time driving and a lot of time ringing. Same hours, opposite
results.

So the first question is not "who do I visit?" but "where can I visit many of them
at once?" Geography decides your real capacity. A territory drawn well can double
your buzzers without a single extra hour.

### Zoning and routing: maximise buzzers per km

**Zoning** is dividing your territory into blocks you can cover in a half-day. Each
zone is small enough that you can walk or short-drive between doors inside it. You
do not mix zones in a day; you take one zone and ring it through.

**Routing** is the order you visit inside a zone. The goal is the shortest path that
touches every stop, not a random walk. Plan the route the night before, start at the
far edge, and work back toward home or your next anchor, so you never double back.

The measure that matters is **buzzers per kilometre** — how many doors you ring per
unit of driving. A day of 8 buzzers over 120 km is poor. The same 8 buzzers over 15
km is excellent. Chase the ratio, not the count.

### Temporal patterns: when companies are most receptive

A door is not only a place; it is a time. The same company answers differently at
10am on a Tuesday than at 4pm on a Friday.

General patterns, to test on your own patch:

- **Mid-morning (9:30–11:30)** is often best: the day has started, the inbox is
  not yet a crisis, people are at their desks.
- **Early afternoon (2:00–4:00)** is a second window after lunch.
- **Monday mornings** are crowded with internal meetings; avoid the first hours.
- **Friday afternoons** people are checked out; a poor time to ask for anything.
- **Month-end and quarter-end** the finance and decision people are buried; the
  users are free, so ring users then and deciders earlier.

There is no universal clock. Keep a note of when you actually get through and when
you get turned away, and let your own data set your hours.

### Weekly and daily planning

Territory work is won on Sunday and lost on Wednesday. Plan the week so each day
owns one zone, and plan the day the night before.

A weekly shape:

| Day | Zone | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Tuesday | North block | Tier 2 targeted buzzers, mid-morning |
| Wednesday | Industrial estate A | Tier 1 ABM visits, pre-booked |
| Thursday | Town centre | Tier 3 standardised coverage |
| Friday | Follow-ups + admin | No driving; calls and paperwork |

Leave Friday for follow-ups and admin so the week ends with the pipeline tidy, not
with a stack of unlogged visits. Never plan a day that crosses two far-apart zones;
that is where the kilometres eat the buzzers.

### The buzzer density principle

Density is the whole game. Ringing in a concentrated zone means short hops between
doors, so more of your day is spent at buzzers and less in the car. It has a second
benefit: when you ring five companies on one street, you become a known face in
that area. The gatekeeper who saw you Tuesday tells the one on Wednesday, "he's the
one from the estate." Familiarity travels a short distance fast, and a known face
opens doors a stranger cannot.

Low density is the enemy. One door in one town is a cold call with a drive attached.
High density turns one trip into ten buzzers and a reputation.

### Planning tools: maps, CRM, routing apps

You cannot hold a territory in your head. Use tools that hold it for you.

- **Maps** to draw zones and eyeball the shortest route between stops.
- **CRM** (Customer Relationship Management: the system that stores your accounts,
  contacts, and visit history) to see where your targets are and what you last did
  with each.
- **Routing apps** to order the day's stops into the shortest path.

The right tool is the one you actually use. A marked-up paper map beats a CRM you
never open. But the moment your list grows past what paper can hold, a CRM with a
map view becomes the difference between a planned territory and a lucky one.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Territory | The area a seller is responsible for | "His territory is the north industrial belt." |
| Zoning | Dividing a territory into coverable blocks | "We zoned the city into four half-day blocks." |
| Routing | The order of stops on a day | "The route starts at the far edge and works back." |
| Buzzer density | Buzzers per unit of distance | "High density means short hops and more ringing." |
| Buzzers per km | The ratio that measures a day | "Fifteen kilometres for eight buzzers is a good ratio." |
| CRM | System storing accounts, contacts, visit history | "The CRM map showed the cluster we missed." |

## A field note

The problem every territory seller feels — the shortest route through many stops —
is famous in mathematics. A handbook for travelling salesmen mentioned it as early
as 1832, but it was first studied in the 1930s in Vienna by Karl Menger, who
called it the "messenger problem" (*Botenproblem*): find the shortest route
connecting a set of points with known distances between each pair. The name
"travelling salesman problem" first appeared in a 1949 RAND Corporation report by
the mathematician Julia Robinson. It became one of the most studied problems in
computer science, and its generalisation — the vehicle routing problem — is what
modern logistics and territory-routing software actually solve. The buzzer seller's
daily route is a small, human version of a problem that has occupied mathematicians
for a century. (Source: Wikipedia, "Travelling salesman problem.")

## Exercises

1. **Draw your zones.** Take a map of your patch and divide it into four half-day
   zones. Mark each with a name. No zone may require more than a 20-minute drive to
   cross.
2. **Ratio audit.** Log one real day: total buzzers and total kilometres driven.
   Compute buzzers per km. Then re-plan the same visits by density and compute the
   ratio you *could* have had.
3. **Temporal test.** For two weeks, note the hour of every visit and whether you
   got through. Find your own best two-hour window and rebuild your day around it.
4. **Weekly shape.** Draft a week where each day owns one zone and Friday is
   follow-ups and admin with no driving. Check that no day crosses two far-apart
   zones.

## Summary

- Geography is a strategic variable: distance is time you are not ringing, so the
  map decides your real capacity.
- Zone your territory into half-day blocks and route each day's stops into the
  shortest path; measure the day in buzzers per kilometre.
- Companies are more receptive mid-morning and early afternoon; avoid Monday's
  first hours and Friday's last, and let your own log set your clock.
- Plan the week one zone per day and the day the night before; keep Friday for
  follow-ups and admin.
- Buzzer density is the principle: concentrated zones mean short hops, more
  ringing, and a familiar face that opens doors.
- AgentBridge's map tools plan the route and draw the service area from a plain
  sentence, so you spend the day ringing instead of driving.
