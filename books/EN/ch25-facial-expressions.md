# Facial Expressions and Micro-Expressions

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Explain in plain words why the face moves faster than conscious thought.
- Name the basic facial expressions research found across cultures, and the one that was contested.
- Recognise a micro-expression by what it looks like and how long it lasts.
- Read a price moment and a contempt moment in a live negotiation.
- Control your own leakage at the three moments where it costs you money.

## Key concepts

### The face is the fastest channel you have

The face is wired differently from the rest of the body. Much of its movement is driven by the older, emotional parts of the brain — what people loosely call the **limbic system** — rather than by the deliberate, planning parts. The practical result: a facial reaction can start before you have decided to have it, and before you can stop it.

This cuts both ways, and you must hold both halves in mind. **Their face leaks** — what they really think of your price can appear before they say anything. **Your face leaks too** — your surprise at their budget, your irritation at a rude receptionist, your relief at a yes, all visible and all before you speak.

Most sellers train only the first half. The second half is worth more. A seller who controls their own face keeps their negotiating position; a seller who reads faces badly but stays neutral can still correct the read later.

### The cross-cultural basic emotions

In the late 1960s Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen showed photographs of American faces to the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, who had almost no contact with Western media. Asked to match the photos to stories, the Fore chose the same expressions for the same emotions that Americans did — the finding that made the claim that a set of expressions is universal rather than learned.

| Emotion | The tell |
|---------|---------|
| Anger | Brows lowered and drawn together, narrowed eyes, tight lips |
| Disgust | Wrinkled nose, upper lip raised |
| Fear | Brows raised and drawn together, upper eyelids raised, mouth stretched |
| Joy | Crow's-feet at the eyes, cheeks lifted, mouth corners back and up |
| Sadness | Inner brows raised and drawn together, mouth corners down |
| Surprise | Brows raised high, eyes wide, jaw drops open |

Two honest qualifications. In that original cross-cultural work **contempt** was less clear — there was some evidence for it and some not, and it was added to the standard list later. And other researchers have since challenged strict universality, arguing that context and culture shape expressions more than Ekman allowed. Both things are true at once: there is a strong shared core, and it is not a magic decoder ring.

Note the difference in the joy row. A real smile engages the muscle around the eye and crinkles it; a polite smile moves only the mouth. The first is a **Duchenne smile**, named after Duchenne de Boulogne, a nineteenth-century French neurologist who studied facial muscles by stimulating them electrically. The eye crinkle is very hard to fake on purpose, and it is the most reliable single facial cue a seller will ever use.

### What a micro-expression is

A **micro-expression** is a full facial expression that fires and disappears very fast. Ekman's own materials describe it as lasting half a second or less, with the classic figure being about one twenty-fifth of a second — roughly forty milliseconds. An ordinary "macro" expression lasts between half a second and four seconds.

Three properties matter for selling. They are **involuntary** — people cannot stop them and usually do not know they happened. They often show **the emotion being concealed** — a flash of anger under a polite "that is interesting" is not a mistake, it is the actual feeling arriving first. And they are often **partial**: sometimes the whole face flashes, but more often only one region moves, a corner of the mouth dropping on one side or the nose wrinkling for an instant.

Full cinematic flashes are rare in ordinary conversation. Most of what you will read at a buzzer is a small, fast, partial leak. That is good news: partial leaks are easier to spot and easier to train.

### How to recognise one: four rules

Never read a single facial movement alone.

| Rule | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| Baseline first | Learn how this person looks at rest. Deviation from baseline is the signal; the movement itself is not. |
| Find the trigger | A leak has a cause. Ask what you just said or showed. No trigger, no claim. |
| Read clusters | Face plus voice plus hands plus feet. One cue is a guess; three pointing the same way is evidence. |
| Check congruence | Do the words and the face agree? Incongruence is the finding, whatever the face meant. |

Ekman and Friesen built the tool behind all this: the **Facial Action Coding System** (FACS), published in 1978, which breaks the face into individual muscle actions called action units and describes each expression as a combination of them. Learning FACS properly takes months. You do not need it. You need the four rules and the tells in the table above.

### Practical application: three moments worth watching

**The price moment.** Say the number, then stop. Do not fill the silence. Watch the face for two seconds for a quick brow draw, a lip press, a swallow, a glance down at the paper — then listen. If the face said pain and the words said "that is reasonable", you have a hidden objection and you open it now: "I said the number and you went quiet a second. Is it the amount or the timing?"

**The competitor mention.** Name the competitor you are up against and watch. Contempt shows as one corner of the mouth lifting, often one-sided, sometimes with a small head shake. That is a gift: they have already decided. Ask "What do you get from them that we do not?" and you will hear the real gap in one sentence.

**The surprise test.** Tell them something you have never told them — a figure, a case, a feature. Watch for genuine surprise held for a beat versus polite nothing. Genuine surprise means you have information they needed. Polite nothing means you are late to the conversation and should stop pitching and start asking.

### Controlling your own face

Your leakage costs money in three places: hearing their budget, where a visible deflation hands them a discount before you have negotiated; hearing an objection you have heard a hundred times, where the micro-sigh or jaw tighten tells them they are wasting your time; and being turned away at the door, where you carry the rejection to the next door and the next.

Four drills that work:

- **The two-second hold.** After anything they say, wait two seconds before responding. The pause covers the flash and buys time to choose your face.
- **Neutral resting face.** Find your neutral in a mirror: jaw loose, tongue off the roof of the mouth, brows flat, lips closed but not pressed. Practise returning to it on a cue word.
- **Breathe out through the objection.** A long exhale physically relaxes the brow and the jaw, and slows your voice, which reads as control.
- **Think a neutral thought.** When a flash is coming, occupy the mind with something flat — counting tiles, the drive home. A busy mind leaks less than a suppressed one. Suppression shows; distraction does not.

### The honest limits

Do not overclaim this skill, in the book or in the room. Untrained people are poor lie-detectors and the training industry has oversold them. The U.S. Transportation Security Administration's SPOT programme, which trained officers to read nervous behaviour at airports, was criticised for lacking solid controlled validation, and a 2007 report put untrained lie-catchers at about the accuracy of a coin flip. Ekman's taxonomy and methods have drawn criticism from other researchers, and some of his later work was not put through peer review.

What survives the criticism, and what this chapter teaches: facial expressions are real, partly universal and worth reading; baseline, trigger, cluster and congruence are a disciplined method, not a superpower; reading a face gives you a **question to ask**, never a conclusion to act on; and controlling your own face is more reliably valuable than reading theirs.

Ekman's work inspired the television series *Lie to Me* (2009), with Ekman as scientific adviser, and Paul Ekman International (founded 2010) sells training in reading emotion. Television made it look like mind-reading. It is careful attention with a method — and the seller who uses it modestly beats the seller who uses it confidently.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|------|---------------|--------------------|
| Limbic system | The older, emotional brain regions that drive fast facial reactions | "The face is limbic, so it moves before the mind decides." |
| Micro-expression | A facial expression lasting about a fortieth to a half second | "He denied it; the micro-expression said otherwise." |
| Macro-expression | An ordinary expression lasting half a second to four seconds | "Her surprise was macro, so it was probably social." |
| Leakage | Emotion showing despite an attempt to hide it | "Watch for leakage in the mouth after controlled eye contact." |
| Mini expression | A leak limited to one region of the face | "Only one corner of his mouth dropped — a mini expression." |
| Baseline | How this person looks at rest | "Never read a face without a baseline." |
| Trigger | The thing said or shown that caused the leak | "If I cannot name the trigger, I cannot claim the read." |
| Cluster | Several cues pointing the same way | "One cue is a guess; a cluster is evidence." |
| FACS | Facial Action Coding System, the muscle-by-muscle map of expressions | "FACS is for researchers; the four rules are for sellers." |
| Duchenne smile | A real smile that crinkles the eye | "Mouth only is polite; eye crinkle is joy." |

## AgentBridge in action

You cannot read a room you do not understand. If your attention is spent searching for something to say, you are not watching their face. Load the content beforehand so your attention is free.

> **What are the top three trends in small-business e-commerce this year? Give me sources.**

AgentBridge searches recent articles and gives you the key trends in plain language, each with a source you can open. Do this on the way to a visit and you walk in with three things worth saying that are not about your product. That buys two advantages at once: the customer relaxes because you are not pitching, and your eyes are free to watch the face while you talk. Schedule the same thing as a daily digest and you never walk into any room cold.

**Sales angle:** every prepared sentence is attention you get back for reading faces — and a seller who opens with three facts about the customer's market sees more, because they are not thinking about themselves.

## A field note

In the late 1960s Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen travelled to Papua New Guinea and showed photographs of American faces to the Fore people, who were pre-literate and had almost no exposure to Western media. Asked to match the photos to stories, the Fore chose the same expressions for the same emotions that Americans did. The paper, "Constants across cultures in the face and emotion," was published in 1971 and became the foundation of the universality claim — though the same study left **contempt** unresolved, with only preliminary evidence for it. Ekman and Friesen then built the Facial Action Coding System, published in 1978, mapping every facial muscle movement into coded action units. Ekman's own materials describe a micro-expression as lasting half a second or less, with the classic figure around one twenty-fifth of a second. The honest footnote: his methods and taxonomy have been contested, and a 2007 report on untrained lie-detection put accuracy near chance — which is exactly why the disciplined version of this skill is about clusters and questions, not certainty. (Sources: Wikipedia, "Paul Ekman"; paulekman.com, "Micro-expressions".)

## Exercises

1. **Baseline map.** Before your next meeting, spend five minutes talking about nothing — the weather, the drive, the coffee. While you do, write down how this person's face sits at rest: brows, mouth corners, eye tension. In the meeting, read every reaction against that note, not against a book.
2. **The pause drill.** Take a video of a negotiation or a pitch interview. Play it, pause at every number mentioned, and study the frozen face for two seconds. Name one thing you see. Then unpause and listen to what they say, and check whether face and words agreed. Ten pauses a day for a week changes the eye.
3. **The price moment, live.** In your next real conversation, state your price and force yourself into total silence for two full seconds while you watch. Then ask: "I noticed something when I said the number — is it the amount or the timing?" Count how often the silence reveals what the answer would not.
4. **Your own face on camera.** Record sixty seconds of yourself answering three things: your price, a common objection, and a rejection. Watch it with the sound off and mark every frame where your face gave an emotion away. Re-record the same three using the two-second hold and the neutral resting face. Keep going until the sound-off version tells you nothing.

## Summary

- The face is driven by fast, emotional wiring, so it moves before the mind decides — theirs and yours.
- A core set of expressions appears across cultures; contempt was contested, and universality is not a decoder ring.
- A micro-expression lasts about a fortieth of a second and often shows only the emotion being hidden, sometimes in one region only.
- Read with four rules — baseline, trigger, cluster, congruence — and treat every read as a question to ask, never a conclusion.
- The real money is in controlling your own face at the price moment, the repeated objection and the rejection, and in preparing your talking points so your attention is free to watch the room.
