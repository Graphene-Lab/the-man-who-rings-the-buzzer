# Value-Based Selling: Sell the Value, Not the Product

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Explain value-based selling (VBS) and how it differs from feature and price selling.
- Reframe a pitch around the economic value the buyer gains, not the product.
- Build a clear value statement in plain numbers.
- Write a value statement for a product you actually sell.

## Key concepts

Value-based selling means pricing and pitching on the **value the customer
receives**, not on the product's features or on what the last seller charged. The
question you answer is not "what is it?" or "what does it cost?" but "what is it
worth to you?" A product that saves a firm ten hours a week is worth roughly the
wages of those ten hours, every week, for as long as it works. That number — not
your cost, not your competitor's price — is the anchor.

### Foundations: VBS in industrial marketing

Value-based selling comes from business-market strategy. The core idea, set out by
James C. Anderson, James A. Narus, and Wouter van Rossum in the *Harvard
Business Review* article "Customer Value Propositions in Business Markets"
(March 2006), is that a seller should offer a **differentiated value
proposition**: a clear statement of the economic value the customer gains from
your offer compared with the next-best choice. It is not a slogan. It is a
quantified case.

Three selling styles sit on a ladder, and most sellers are stuck on the bottom
rung:

| Style | What you sell | Buyer hears | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feature selling | What the product does | "So what?" | Weak — features are not benefits |
| Price selling | How cheap it is | "Cheaper than what? And why?" | Weak — invites a price war |
| Value-based selling | What it's worth to you | "This pays for itself" | Strong — justifies the price |

Feature selling lists what the thing does. Price selling competes on cost and
trains the buyer to wait for a discount. Value-based selling shifts the ground to
the buyer's own economics, where a fair price is easy to accept because the gain
is bigger than the cost.

### The pitch must articulate value for the customer

The most common pitch mistake is talking about yourself. "Our software has an
AI engine, a dashboard, and a mobile app." The buyer does not buy features; they
buy the change in their own numbers. Every feature must be translated, out loud,
into a value the buyer can feel.

The translation runs in one direction: feature → benefit → value.

- Feature: "It automates invoicing."
- Benefit: "Your staff stop keying invoices by hand."
- Value: "That saves about ten hours a week — roughly 400 euros a week in wages,
  or over 20,000 euros a year."

A pitch that stops at the feature asks the buyer to do the maths. A pitch that
finishes the sentence does the maths for them and hands them a number they can
defend to their boss. That is the whole craft.

To find the value, ask the buyer questions that surface money and pain: How many
hours does this take now? What does an hour cost you? What happens when it goes
wrong — what does an error cost? What do you lose when it is late? Their answers
are the raw material of your value statement. You are not inventing the number;
you are adding up what they told you.

### Building a value statement

A value statement is a short, quantified claim of the buyer's gain. It has four
parts:

1. **The buyer's situation.** "You key 200 invoices a month by hand."
2. **The change you bring.** "We automate that to a five-minute review."
3. **The quantified value.** "That saves about 10 hours a week — around 20,000
   euros a year in labour."
4. **The comparison.** "Against a price of 500 euros a month, you save about
   14,000 euros a year, and break even in about six weeks."

Keep it honest and checkable. Use the buyer's own numbers wherever you can. If
you cannot quantify a gain, do not fake it — say what you can prove and leave the
rest as a softer benefit. A value statement that overreaches is worse than none,
because one wrong number discredits the whole case.

Two related ideas make the case stronger. **Return on investment (ROI)** is the
gain relative to the cost — "you put in 6,000, you save 20,000, that is a strong
return." **Total cost of ownership (TCO)** is the whole cost of running the thing
over time, not just the sticker price — and showing you have thought about TCO
signals that you are honest about what the buyer is really paying. A seller who
can talk about ROI and TCO in the buyer's own numbers sounds like a partner, not
a vendor.

Value-based selling also protects your margin. When you sell on value, a higher
price is defensible because the gain is larger. When you sell on price, you have
no defence except to cut again. The method is not just more persuasive; it is
more profitable.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Value-based selling | Selling on the value to the customer, not price or features | "Sell the 20,000 euros saved, not the software." |
| Value proposition | The quantified case of value the buyer gains | "Your value proposition is the number they can defend." |
| Feature vs benefit | What it does vs what it does for them | "'Automated' is a feature; '10 hours saved' is a benefit." |
| ROI | Return relative to what you spend | "The ROI is strong: save 20,000 on a 6,000 cost." |
| TCO | Total cost of ownership over time | "Talk TCO so the buyer trusts the real price." |
| Break-even | When the savings cover the cost | "You break even in about six weeks." |

## A field note

Value-based selling has a clear academic root in business-market strategy. In
"Customer Value Propositions in Business Markets" (*Harvard Business Review*,
March 2006), James C. Anderson, James A. Narus, and Wouter van Rossum argued
that a seller should offer a differentiated value proposition — a quantified
statement of the economic value the customer gains versus the next-best choice —
rather than competing on product features or price. The article's lasting point
is that the value lives in the customer's numbers, not the seller's brochure.
(Source: Harvard Business Review, March 2006.)

## Exercises

1. **Feature to value.** Take three features of your product and write the full
   chain for each: feature → benefit → a number. If you cannot reach a number,
   find the question that would surface one.
2. **Ask the money questions.** Write the five questions that surface a buyer's
   hours, wage cost, error cost, and lost-time cost. Practise asking them before
   you ever mention price.
3. **Build a value statement.** Write a four-part value statement (situation,
   change, quantified value, comparison) for a real prospect, using their own
   numbers where you have them.
4. **Make the document.** Turn your value statement into a one-page report with
   the savings, the price, the ROI and the break-even. If you use AgentBridge,
   ask for the PDF financial summary and put the buyer's figures into it.

## Summary

- Value-based selling pitches on what the offer is worth to the buyer, not on
  features or price.
- Feature selling and price selling are weak; value selling shifts the ground to
  the buyer's own economics, where a fair price is easy to accept.
- Translate every feature into a number: feature → benefit → value, and let the
  buyer's own answers supply the figures.
- A value statement has four parts — situation, change, quantified value,
  comparison — and must stay honest and checkable.
- Put the value in a document the buyer can verify; a conclusion they reach
  themselves is the strongest close you have.
