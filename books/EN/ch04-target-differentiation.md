# Target Differentiation: Not Every Buzzer Deserves the Same Effort

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Explain why choosing *where* to ring is the first productivity lever in B2B selling.
- Use the four pillars of segmentation — firmographic, technological, behavioural, intent — to sort a list of companies.
- Place every account into a tier: Tier 1 (account-based), Tier 2 (targeted buzzer), Tier 3 (standardised buzzer).
- Treat the Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) as a living document you update, not a poster on the wall.
- Build a priority matrix of ICP fit against intent signal, and write exclusion criteria for who does *not* get a visit.

## Key concepts

The beginner thinks the job is to ring more buzzers. The professional knows the job
is to ring the *right* buzzers harder and the wrong ones not at all. Your time is
the only resource you cannot buy back. Spend it evenly across every company and you
get an even, mediocre result. Spend it unevenly — heavy on the few accounts that
can pay you most, light on the rest — and the same hours return several times the
revenue. This is target differentiation: matching the weight of your effort to the
value of the account.

### Segmentation is the first productivity lever

A thousand companies could, in theory, buy from you. A thousand buzzers is a year
of ringing. Segmentation is how you turn "a thousand" into "the forty that matter."
It means dividing the market into groups that behave alike, so you can treat each
group with a different plan.

Why is this the *first* lever, before your pitch or your product? Because every
later skill is multiplied by where you point it. A perfect pitch to a company that
will never buy returns zero. A rough pitch to a company built to buy returns a
fortune. Fix the aim before you polish the arrow.

The rule behind it is the 80/20 pattern: a small share of accounts produces most of
your revenue. Find that share and ring it hardest.

### The four pillars of segmentation

Four kinds of information sort companies. Read together, they tell you who to chase.

| Pillar | What it measures | Example signal |
|---|---|---|
| Firmographic | The fixed facts of the firm: size, industry, location, revenue, age | "Manufacturing, 50–200 staff, within 30 km." |
| Technological | The stack they run: software, hardware, systems | "Uses an old ERP we can replace." |
| Behavioural | What they actually do: buying history, usage, response | "Bought from us before; opens every email." |
| Intent | The signal they are *ready now*: searches, hiring, funding, requests | "Posted a job for a role we serve." |

Firmographics narrow the field cheaply. Technographics tell you whether your product
even fits their world. Behavioural data separates talkers from buyers. Intent is the
hottest of all: it tells you not just *who* could buy but *when* they are thinking
about it. A cold firmographic match with no intent is a maybe. The same match with a
live intent signal is a door you should ring this week.

### The tiering model

Once sorted, put every account into one of three tiers. The tier decides how much you
spend, not how much you hope.

**Tier 1 — Account-based (ABM).** The vital few. High value, high fit, high intent.
Here you do not just ring; you run a campaign. You research the people, name the
decision makers, customise everything, and follow up for weeks. A single Tier 1
account may be worth more than a hundred cold doors, so a month of work on one is
rational.

**Tier 2 — Targeted buzzer.** Good fit, some intent, decent size. You ring with a
prepared but not fully customised approach. A short research pass, a tailored line,
a couple of follow-ups. This is the engine room of the buzzer: most of your meetings
come from here.

**Tier 3 — Standardised buzzer.** Possible fit, low intent, small size. You ring
with a standard script, no custom research, no scheduled follow-up. The goal is
coverage and the occasional surprise, not depth. If a Tier 3 account shows a strong
intent signal, promote it to Tier 2.

The tiers are not labels of worth; they are budgets of effort. Never let a Tier 3
account eat Tier 1 time.

### The ICP as a living document

The Ideal Customer Profile is a written description of the company that buys most
happily and most often. It is not a guess you make once. It is a document you update
every month from what you learn at the door.

Start with a hypothesis: "Our best customer is a firm of 20–100 staff, in logistics
or manufacturing, within 40 km, that already uses a computer system but no
automation." Then test it. After a month of ringing, look at who said yes, who said
no, and why. Rewrite the profile. The ICP that survives contact with real doors is
worth ten times the one invented in a meeting room.

Write it down. An ICP in your head drifts; an ICP on paper holds you honest. When a
tempting but wrong account walks in, the written profile is what lets you say no.

### The priority matrix: ICP fit × intent signal

To rank a list quickly, score each account on two axes and plot it.

| | Low intent | High intent |
|---|---|---|
| **High ICP fit** | Nurture — Tier 2, watch for a trigger | **Ring now — Tier 1** |
| **Low ICP fit** | Ignore / exclude | Opportunistic — Tier 3, one call only |

Fit tells you the ceiling: how much this company *could* be worth. Intent tells you
the timing: whether now is the moment. The top-right cell — high fit, high intent —
is where you spend your mornings. High fit with no intent is a garden you water
slowly. Low fit with high intent is a trap: they want to buy, but not from you, or
not at a price worth your time.

### Exclusion criteria: who does not get a buzzer

Deciding who to skip is as valuable as deciding who to chase. Write exclusion rules
and apply them before you ever lace up your shoes. Common exclusions:

- Too small to pay a price that covers your cost to serve.
- Wrong industry — your product cannot solve their problem, no matter the pitch.
- Wrong geography — a one-hour drive for a deal worth an hour's margin.
- Bad history — a past account that never paid, or a relationship better left alone.
- No reachable decision maker — you cannot get past a wall that will never open.

A buzzer is physical and costs real time. Exclusion criteria keep that time from
being spent on doors that were never going to open.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Segmentation | Dividing the market into groups that behave alike | "We segmented by size before we rang anyone." |
| Firmographic | The fixed facts of a firm: size, industry, place | "Firmographics cut a thousand names to two hundred." |
| Technographic | The tech a company runs | "Their technographic told us the ERP was replaceable." |
| Intent signal | Evidence a company is ready to buy now | "A job posting was our first intent signal." |
| ABM | Account-based marketing: a custom campaign per big account | "Tier 1 gets a full ABM push, not a cold line." |
| ICP | Ideal Customer Profile: the best-fit company, written down | "The ICP is a living document, not a poster." |
| Exclusion criteria | Rules for who you do not pursue | "Bad payers are on the exclusion list." |

## A field note

The idea behind tiering — that a few causes drive most results — is old and was not
born in a sales manual. The Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto noticed around 1906
that roughly 80% of the land in the Kingdom of Italy was owned by 20% of the
population, a pattern he described in his *Cours d'économie politique* (published
1896–1897). Decades later, in 1941, the management consultant Joseph M. Juran
carried the idea into business as the "vital few and trivial many," and it became the
80/20 rule of thumb that 80% of sales come from 20% of clients. The buzzer seller's
tiering model is Pareto's curve with a plan attached: find the vital few, ring them
like you mean it. (Sources: Wikipedia, "Pareto principle.")

## Exercises

1. **Four-pillar sort.** Take any list of 20 companies. For each, write one signal
   under each pillar — firmographic, technological, behavioural, intent. Mark the
   companies with the most signals in green.
2. **Tier the list.** Put those 20 into Tier 1, 2, 3 using the rule: Tier 1 = high
   fit + high intent, Tier 3 = low fit or no intent, Tier 2 = everything between.
   Write one sentence on why each Tier 1 account earned its tier.
3. **Rewrite the ICP.** Write your current ICP in three lines. Then list three
   things you learned at the door this month that would change it. Rewrite it.
4. **Write your exclusions.** Draft five exclusion criteria for your own patch. Read
   them before your next planning session and cut every account that matches.

## Summary

- Where you ring matters more than how well you pitch; target differentiation is the
  first productivity lever in B2B selling.
- Four pillars sort a market: firmographic (fixed facts), technological (their
  stack), behavioural (what they do), intent (whether they are ready now).
- Three tiers budget your effort: Tier 1 account-based, Tier 2 targeted buzzer,
  Tier 3 standardised buzzer — never let Tier 3 eat Tier 1 time.
- The ICP is a living document you rewrite from real door-learnings, not a guess.
- The priority matrix (ICP fit × intent) puts the top-right cell — high fit, high
  intent — where your mornings go; exclusion criteria keep the wrong doors closed.
- AgentBridge turns a flat account list into a tiered KPI dashboard you can show a
  Tier 1 buyer as proof, not promise.
