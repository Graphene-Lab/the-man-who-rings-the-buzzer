# Eye Contact and the Gaze

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Explain in plain words what **mutual gaze** does to a person and why it feels powerful.
- Hold a gaze that builds trust without tipping into a stare that intimidates.
- Use the eyes to run the turn-taking of a conversation instead of fighting for the floor.
- Adjust your gaze for gender and cultural differences so you do not misfire.
- Practise the gaze in role play until it is automatic at the buzzer.

## Key concepts

### The gaze is the only channel that runs both ways

With your hands you give. With your voice you send. With your eyes you do both at once: you read the other person while they read you. That double direction is why the eyes feel more charged than any other part of the body, and why a seller who has not trained the gaze is exposed in every meeting.

The core event is **mutual gaze** — the moment when both people are looking at each other's eyes at the same time. It is rare in ordinary talk, and when it happens it is noticed. Mutual gaze raises **arousal**: the plain, physical feeling of being switched on, heart a little faster, attention narrowed. Here is the part most sellers get wrong. Arousal is not warmth. It is an amplifier. It turns up whatever the moment is already about. Mutual gaze in a friendly meeting deepens the connection; mutual gaze in a hostile one feels like a threat. The eyes do not create the mood. They intensify the mood that is already there.

This single fact governs everything else in the chapter. You are not trying to "make eye contact." You are trying to keep the moment warm enough that the gaze amplifies trust rather than fear.

### How much is normal

People do not stare. In comfortable conversation each person looks at the other only part of the time, and looks away often. The psychologist Michael Argyle and his colleague Dean described this in 1965 as an **equilibrium**: two people keep adjusting how much they look, so that the shared arousal stays at a level both can tolerate. Look too little and you seem cold or shifty; look too much and you push the arousal past comfort and the other person breaks away or bristles.

Treat the working rule of thumb as roughly half the time, held in short blocks of three to five seconds, then a natural break. That is a guide, not a laboratory number. The point is the shape, not the count: steady, soft, broken — never a fixed beam. A seller who tries to hold the gaze the whole time reads as nervous, aggressive, or rehearsed. The break is not weakness; the break is what makes the hold believable.

### When the gaze builds trust and when it intimidates

The line between warm and threatening is thin and it is crossed by three things: hardness, length, and timing.

A **soft** gaze — eyes relaxed, focus gentle, face loose — reads as interest. A **hard** gaze — eyes narrowed, lids tight, focus locked — reads as a challenge. Length matters: a hold of two or three seconds with a soft face is connection; the same hold with a hard face is dominance. Timing matters most: a gaze that arrives with a smile at greeting opens the door; a gaze that locks on when you are being refused feels like a fight.

Remember what the eyes mean in our oldest inheritance. Among primates a direct, unbroken stare is an aggressive signal, a prelude to conflict. We are not monkeys, but the flicker of that wiring is still there, which is why a stranger holding your eyes too long in a lift makes your skin crawl even when you cannot say why. In many cultures the rule is reversed on purpose: in parts of East Asia and West Africa it is respectful *not* to look a dominant person straight in the eye (Galanti, 2004). So the bumper-sticker rule "good eye contact means honesty" is a narrow Western habit, not a human law. Read the person in front of you, not the poster.

### The gaze runs the conversation

Beyond trust, the eyes are a traffic light. They open and close the channel between two people, and a skilled seller uses them to control who is speaking without ever grabbing for the floor.

Watch how it works. When someone is holding the floor and thinking, they look away — breaking contact gives the mind room to work. When they want to yield, or want a response, they look toward the other person. You can use the same signals on purpose:

- **To keep the floor while you make a point**, let your eyes drift down to your notes or the table for a beat. You hold the turn without staring.
- **To hand the turn over**, lift your eyes, hold them softly on the buyer, and stop talking. The look says "now you."
- **To check you landed**, look for the eyes to come back to yours. Eyes that return mean the message got through; eyes that stay away mean you should ask a question instead of pressing on.

A seller who understands this never talks over a client and never stalls in a silence, because the eyes are telling them whose turn it is.

### Gender and the gaze

There are broad, well-reported differences, and they are averages with huge overlap — treat them as a tendency to watch for, never a certainty about any one person. Across study after study, women, on average, use more eye contact, hold it longer, and read faces more, using the gaze mainly to build rapport. Men, on average, use the gaze more as a status and dominance signal.

The practical consequence at the buzzer is about how a gaze is *received*, not who is right. A man who locks a hard gaze on a woman can cross into "threat" faster than the same gaze between two men. A woman's steady, warm gaze can read as strong rapport where a man might need to add a smile to get the same effect. None of this is a rule you impose; it is a default you watch and correct. If the person in front of you breaks the gaze often and leans back, you are too intense regardless of gender — soften and shorten.

### The gaze at the buzzer

Three moments where the eyes pay or cost you.

**The gatekeeper.** The receptionist decides whether you reach the decision-maker. A warm, brief, soft gaze with a smile says "I am a person, not a flyer." A gaze that slides past them to the boss's office says "you are furniture," and furniture gets you locked out. Give the gatekeeper real eye contact first.

**The greeting.** Open with the eyes before the words: two seconds of soft, warm contact as you say hello. This is the single highest-value gaze of the visit. It sets the arousal warm, so everything that follows is amplified in your favour.

**The price.** Say the number, then hold a soft gaze for two seconds and stop. You are not glaring. You are showing you are comfortable with the number and unafraid of the silence. A seller who drops their eyes when they name the price has just told the buyer the price is too high before the buyer thinks it.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|------|---------------|--------------------|
| Mutual gaze | Both people looking into each other's eyes at once | "A second of mutual gaze said more than my opening line." |
| Arousal | The physical feeling of being switched on | "The eyes raise arousal; they do not decide if it feels good." |
| Gaze equilibrium | The back-and-forth adjustment that keeps looking comfortable | "She looked away, so I looked away too — we found the balance." |
| Fixed stare | A hard, unbroken gaze that reads as threat | "His fixed stare made me want to end the meeting." |
| Soft gaze | Relaxed, gentle focus that reads as interest | "A soft gaze with a smile opened the door." |
| Turn-taking signal | Using the eyes to open or close the speaking turn | "I lifted my eyes and stopped; the look handed her the floor." |
| Gaze aversion | Looking away, often to think or to show respect | "Her gaze aversion was not dishonesty; she was thinking." |
| Baseline | How this person normally uses their eyes | "He rarely holds a gaze, so a long look from him meant something." |

## AgentBridge in action

A steady gaze is not a trick of the face. It is the by-product of an uncluttered mind. If you are walking in wondering what you promised the last client, or whether the order shipped, your eyes will drift and search because your working memory is busy. Offload the recall before you ring, and your eyes are free to do their job.

## A field note

In 1989 psychologists Kellerman, Lewis and Laird ran a study called "Looking and loving: The effects of mutual gaze on feelings of romantic love" in the *Journal of Research in Personality*. Strangers of the opposite sex were paired off and asked, among other things, to hold two minutes of sustained mutual gaze. Compared with control pairs, those who gazed reported a significant rise in feelings of passionate love, dispositional love, and simple liking for their partner. The authors read it through **self-perception theory**: the act of gazing made them feel the feeling, not the other way round — "I am looking into their eyes, so I must like them." The honest caveats are large: it was a small lab study in a romantic frame, not a sales meeting, and it does not mean staring at a buyer makes them love you. What it does establish is the load-bearing idea of this chapter — the gaze does not just reflect how people feel, it helps create it. (Sources: Kellerman, Lewis & Laird, 1989, *Journal of Research in Personality*; via ScienceDirect and APA PsycNet.)

## Exercises

1. **The three-second hold.** In your next three conversations, hold a soft gaze for a slow count of three at each greeting, then break naturally. Count the seconds in your head the first two times so you learn the length. By the third conversation you should not be counting any more — the block should feel normal, not forced.
2. **The yield-and-take drill.** With a partner, talk for two minutes about a work topic and use only your eyes to manage the turns: look down to keep the floor, look up and stop to hand it over, watch for the eyes to return to check you landed. No verbal cues like "your turn." Afterward ask your partner whether the handovers felt smooth or awkward.
3. **The cold gatekeeper role play.** Have a partner play a bored, unhelpful receptionist. Practise opening with two seconds of warm, soft eye contact plus a smile, and keep the gaze soft even when they brush you off. The drill fails if your eyes harden when you are refused. Repeat until you can be turned down and still look warm.
4. **Watch it with the sound off.** Record one of the role plays above. Play it back with no sound and judge only the eyes: does the gaze read as warm, neutral, or threatening? Mark the moments it slips. Re-record and fix them. The sound-off pass shows you what the buyer sees before they hear a word.

## Summary

- Mutual gaze raises arousal, and arousal amplifies the mood that is already there — warm if warm, threat if hostile.
- Comfortable people do not stare: aim for roughly half the time in soft three-to-five-second blocks, with natural breaks.
- The line between trust and intimidation is hardness, length and timing; a soft gaze with a smile opens, a hard locked stare fights.
- The eyes run turn-taking — look down to keep the floor, look up and stop to hand it over, watch for the eyes to return to check you landed.
- Gender and culture shift how a gaze is received; read the person, not the rule, and prepare your facts with the always-there assistant so your eyes are free to build trust.
