# Handling Objections: Techniques and Simulations

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Sort objections into simple, moderate and major, and match a technique to
  each.
- Tell a true objection from a smokescreen with one question.
- Answer with reframe, negotiate or prove instead of arguing.
- Read the face during an objection — baseline, clusters, and the limits of
  what you can claim.
- Turn "send me an email" and "not the right time" into a fixed next step.
- Build and drill a personal objection-handling playbook.

## Key concepts

An objection is not a refusal. It is a request for more reason. A prospect
who objects is still in the conversation; one who stops objecting has left it.
The buzzer opened the door, discovery found the need — the objection is where
the sale actually starts being tested. Expect it. Welcome it. Answer it with a
method, not adrenaline.

### Types of objections

| Type | What it is | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | A misunderstanding or small doubt: "Does it work with our system?" | Information — answer and move on |
| Moderate | A real concern: price, timing, trust, a favoured competitor | Reframe, prove or negotiate |
| Major | No budget, no authority, no need | Qualify out, or re-route to the real decision-maker |

Sort before you answer. A simple objection answered with a reframe sounds
evasive; a major one answered with information wastes your breath.

Then test for the **smokescreen** — the first stated reason hiding the real
one. The test is one question, asked lightly: "If we solved that today, would
you be ready to move?" If the answer is yes, you found the real objection.
If they produce a new reason, the first one was smoke — keep going until you
reach the reason that survives the question.

### Three response techniques

**Reframe.** Change the frame without fighting the facts. The objection stays
true and stops mattering. "You say we are expensive. Expensive compared with
what? Against what the delays cost you last quarter, this is the cheap
option." A reframe never says the prospect is wrong; it gives the same facts
a bigger picture.

**Negotiate.** Trade, never concede. Give on scope, terms or timing; take on
price, commitment or timeline. "We can start with the smaller rollout at the
lower price — if the pilot hits the numbers, we sign for the full year." A
discount given for nothing teaches the customer that your first price was
fake. Every give gets a take.

**Prove.** Evidence: a live demo, a comparison, a case study, a trial. Use
prove when the objection is a doubt about truth, not a difference of interest.
"You don't believe it fits in an hour? Give me your messiest report and I will
show you on screen, now." Proof ends arguments that words only prolong.

Match technique to type: simple — tell; moderate — reframe, then prove, then
negotiate if price is genuinely short; major — qualify honestly. Arguing is
never on the list, because arguing does not convert; it only hardens.

### Reading the objection

A **micro-expression** is a brief, involuntary facial movement — a fraction
of a second — that shows an emotion before the person can control it. Use
the idea with care: the science of reading them precisely is contested, and no
single twitch should decide how you play a deal. What works in the room is
simpler. Establish a **baseline** early: how does this person look and sound
when they are relaxed and agreeing? Then watch for change, and only in
clusters — face, voice and posture moving together.

| Cue | Possible meaning | Do not conclude |
|---|---|---|
| Jaw tightens as the price lands | discomfort with the number | that they cannot pay |
| Gaze drops when asked to decide | hesitation, maybe about authority | that they are lying |
| Arms cross after the demo | checking, or cold room | that the demo failed |
| Voice goes flat and formal | switching to a guarded register | that the deal is dead |

When the cluster appears, do not interpret — ask. "You went quiet when I said
the number. What is your honest read on it?" The cue's real job is to tell
you when to ask a question you would otherwise skip.

### "Send me an email"

The most common polite exit in business. It usually means: I do not want to
say no to your face. Do not fight it and do not obey it blindly. Agree, then
pin every detail: the address, the content, the date, the trigger. "Happy to.
What address should it go to? I will send the comparison we discussed — and
if the numbers look right to you, would the first week of November work for a
decision?" The email is not the sale; it is the bridge. An exit turned into a
scheduled step is still a step. And send only what you agreed in the room —
the value was the meeting, and a fresh brochure is how deals go to a quiet
inbox to die.

### "Not the right time"

Often true. Which is exactly why it must be managed, not accepted. Ask for the
time: "When would the right time be?" Write down their answer, not yours.
Then shrink the ask: not a decision, a fifteen-minute check-in. "Then let us
not decide anything. The third of February, fifteen minutes, I bring the
pilot numbers and you can still say no." A parked deal with a date beats a
warm maybe with none. The danger is not the delay; it is the follow-up that
never happens.

### Simulations

Objection handling is a reflex, and reflexes are built by repetition, not
reading. Run **objection tennis**: a partner serves objections at random from
a deck; you return each one with a named technique — reframe, negotiate or
prove — in under thirty seconds. No contradictions, no "but." Two-minute
rounds, then debrief: which technique did you use, did it fit the type, did
you argue anywhere? Then the harder game: the partner hides the real
objection behind a smokescreen and you must reach it with the "if we solved
that" question before time runs out. Drill weekly until the three techniques
answer before your nerves do.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Objection | A stated reason not to move — a request for more reason | "Her first objection was really a question." |
| Smokescreen | A surface reason hiding the real one | "Price was smoke; authority was the fire." |
| Reframe | Put the same facts in a bigger picture | "He reframed 'expensive' against the cost of doing nothing." |
| Negotiate (trade) | Give only in exchange | "She traded scope for a signature, never price for nothing." |
| Prove | Answer doubt with evidence | "The live demo proved what the slides could not." |
| Micro-expression | A split-second involuntary facial signal | "His micro-expression said the number stung — so she asked." |
| Baseline | How the person looks when relaxed and agreeing | "Without a baseline, every cue is guesswork." |
| Parked deal | A delayed deal with a fixed return date | "The parked deal reopened in February and closed in March." |

## AgentBridge in action

The price objection is where presentations die and evidence lives. When the
buyer says "too expensive," do not defend the price — widen the frame to
total cost, with researched proof:

> Compare these two printers for a small office: price, running cost and
> reliability.

The agent researches both products and lays out a clear side-by-side
comparison, with the links so you can check every figure. Ask for the total
cost of ownership, not just the sticker price — and hand the comparison
across the table while the objection is still warm. "Too expensive" stops
being a wall and becomes a spreadsheet you both read. The same move works
against any competitor claim: research it live, cite it, let the prospect
watch the evidence arrive.

**Sales angle:** the agent turns "too expensive" from a full stop into a
comparison — prove, never argue.

## A field note

The most-used objection-handling book in history never mentions objections. In
October 1936 Dale Carnegie published *How to Win Friends and Influence
People* with Simon & Schuster, and its rules are the ancestors of every reframe
taught today: "The only way to get the best of an argument is to avoid it."
"Show respect for the other person's opinions. Never say 'You're wrong.'"
"Start with questions to which the other person will answer yes." Eighty
years of objection-handling courses have mostly been these three lines in a
suit — because head-on contradiction hardens a "no," while agreement and a
changed frame leave room to move. (Source: Wikipedia, "How to Win Friends and
Influence People.")

## Exercises

1. **Top ten, sorted.** Write the ten objections you actually hear. Mark
   each simple, moderate or major, and name the technique that fits. Notice
   which type you meet most and which one you handle worst.
2. **Smokescreen hunt.** Role-play with a partner who hides a real objection
   (authority, a bad past experience, a rival vendor) behind a surface one.
   Use "If we solved that today, would you be ready to move?" to reach the
   real reason in under four minutes.
3. **Build the playbook.** For your top five objections, write one page each:
   the reframe line, the proof asset (demo, case study, comparison), and the
   trade you would offer. Drill one page per week until you can answer
   without notes.
4. **Pin the exit.** Practise the "send me an email" drill: every round must
   end with an address, an agreed content, a date and a decision trigger. If
   any one is missing, the round is lost.

## Summary

- An objection is a request for more reason; silence is the worse signal.
- Sort first — simple, moderate, major — and test every first reason for
  smoke with one question.
- Reframe changes the picture, negotiate trades, prove ends doubt; arguing
  hardens everything.
- Read the face as a prompt to ask, never as a verdict: baseline first,
  clusters only.
- "Send me an email" and "not now" must end in a date and a trigger, or they
  ended the deal.
