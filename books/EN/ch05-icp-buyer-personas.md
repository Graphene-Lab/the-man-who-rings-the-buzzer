# ICP, Buyer Personas, and Mapping the Roles

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Define the Ideal Customer Profile (the company) and the buyer persona (the person) and tell them apart.
- Research a target company and read its need signals before you ring.
- Map the four roles at a company: gatekeeper, influencer, decision maker, budget holder.
- Describe the buying coalition — who starts, who sponsors, who pays, who uses, who can kill the deal.
- Build and prioritise a contact list and walk in with a pre-visit checklist.

## Key concepts

The buzzer is not aimed at a company. It is aimed at a person inside a company —
but a company is not one person. Big buying decisions are made by a group, and most
sellers lose because they pitch to whoever answers the door instead of the people
who actually decide. This chapter is about learning to see the group before you walk
in, so you ring the right buzzer and ask for the right name.

### The ICP and the buyer persona

Two ideas, often confused, work together.

The **Ideal Customer Profile** describes the *company*: the firm that buys most
happily and most often. It is made of firmographic and technological facts — size,
industry, systems, location. It answers: "Which companies should I target?"

The **buyer persona** describes the *person* inside that company who buys. It is
made of role, worries, goals, and language. It answers: "Who do I talk to, and what
does that person care about?"

A persona is not a real person; it is a composite you build from many real
conversations. A typical persona: "The Operations Manager, 35–50, hates manual data
entry, fears looking foolish in front of the boss, reads for one minute on a phone,
cares about saving time more than saving money." When you know the persona, you know
what to say and what to avoid before you open your mouth.

### Company research and need signals

Before you ring, spend ten minutes learning the company. You are not looking for
everything; you are looking for *need signals* — signs that they have the problem you
solve.

A need signal is anything that hints at pain: a job posting for a role you serve, a
clunky website, a complaint in a review, a new branch opening, a funding round that
means budget, a system they run that you know is slow. Each signal gives you a
reason to call that is about *them*, not about your product. "I saw you're hiring
three warehouse clerks — is the paperwork keeping up?" is a door-opener. "Can I
tell you about our software?" is not.

Research is a skill and a trap. The skill is finding one good signal and stopping.
The trap is reading for an hour and learning nothing you can use. Set a timer.

### Mapping the four roles

Every company you enter has, at minimum, four roles around a purchase. You must know
which is which.

| Role | Who they are | What they do to you |
|---|---|---|
| Gatekeeper | Reception, assistant, the person at the buzzer | Controls access; can block or help you |
| Influencer | The user or expert whose opinion the buyer trusts | Shapes what "good" looks like |
| Decision maker | The person who says yes or no | Chooses between you and the alternatives |
| Budget holder | The person who signs the money | Releases the funds; often not the decider |

The most common mistake is treating the decision maker as the budget holder. They
are often different people. A manager may decide to buy and a finance director must
approve the spend. If you never reach the budget holder, a "yes" can still die.

The gatekeeper is not your enemy. The receptionist who rings ahead for you is worth
more than a forced meeting. Treat the gatekeeper as a person with their own job to
protect, and they become a route instead of a wall.

### The buying coalition

Big purchases are not made by one person but by a coalition — a group with
different stakes. Learn to name the five positions in it.

- **Who starts it.** The person who first feels the pain and raises the idea. Often
  a user, not a boss.
- **Who sponsors it.** The senior person who backs it and gives it air cover.
- **Who pays.** The budget holder who signs the cheque.
- **Who uses it.** The people who live with the product every day.
- **Who can kill it.** The person who needs no reason to stop it — IT, legal,
  finance, a sceptical partner. One veto is enough.

Your job is not to convince one person. It is to give each position what it needs:
the starter a reason to push, the sponsor a business case, the payer a return, the
user an easier life, and the killer an answer to their fear. A seller who only
impresses the sponsor loses to a seller who also disarms the killer.

### Building and prioritising the list

Turn the map into a working list. For each target company, write the names you can
find for each role, and rank the contacts by how much access and influence they
carry. Start with the person who can open the most doors — often the influencer or
the starter, because they have the pain and will talk. Use the decision maker and
budget holder as your destination, not your first call.

Prioritise the list the same way you tiered accounts: high fit and high intent at
the top. A named contact with a live need signal beats an anonymous "to whom it may
concern" every time.

### The pre-visit checklist

Before you walk to any buzzer, confirm these. If you cannot fill them, do more
research or pick another target.

- One need signal I can quote about this company.
- The name of at least one person to ask for.
- Which role that person holds (gatekeeper, influencer, decider, budget).
- The one thing I want from this visit (a meeting, an intro, a yes to a next step).
- What I will say in the first ten seconds.
- The killer I have not yet reached, and how I will get to them.

A visit without a name is a cold call with extra steps. A visit with a name, a
signal, and a goal is a professional approach that gets through.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| ICP | The ideal *company* profile | "The ICP is 20–100 staff in logistics." |
| Buyer persona | A composite of the *person* who buys | "The persona fears looking slow to the boss." |
| Need signal | A sign the company has the problem you solve | "A job ad was our need signal." |
| Gatekeeper | Controls who reaches the decision makers | "The receptionist is the gatekeeper." |
| Influencer | Shapes the buyer's opinion | "The warehouse lead is the real influencer." |
| Budget holder | Signs the money | "We closed with the decider but the budget holder killed it." |
| Buying coalition | The group that decides together | "Five people sat in the buying coalition." |

## A field note

The idea that big sales are won by understanding the whole buying group — not by a
clever line — is the core finding of the largest study ever run on selling. Neil
Rackham spent twelve years, with a team of thirty researchers, observing more than
35,000 sales calls across over twenty countries, funded by companies including
Xerox and IBM. The study, published as *SPIN Selling*, found that the traits that
win small sales do not win large, complex ones, and that what matters in big deals
is understanding the many people and needs around the decision. The buzzer seller's
"buying coalition" is that finding made practical: map the group before you ring.
(Source: Wikipedia, "Neil Rackham.")

## Exercises

1. **Persona draft.** Write one buyer persona for your best-fit company: role, age
   range, biggest worry, biggest goal, the one sentence that would make them listen.
2. **Signal hunt.** Pick three target companies. For each, find one public need
   signal you could quote in your opening line. Write the line.
3. **Role map.** For one target, name the four roles — gatekeeper, influencer,
   decision maker, budget holder — and mark which you can reach today and which you
   cannot. Plan how to reach the missing one.
4. **Coalition kill-test.** List the five coalition positions for a real deal. Name
   the "who can kill it" person and write the one answer that disarms their fear.

## Summary

- The ICP describes the company; the buyer persona describes the person — you need
  both, and they are not the same thing.
- Research for one usable need signal, not everything; a signal gives you a
  reason-to-call that is about them, not your product.
- Four roles sit at every door: gatekeeper, influencer, decision maker, budget
  holder — and the decider is often not the one who signs.
- The buying coalition has five positions: who starts, who sponsors, who pays, who
  uses, who can kill it; a single veto ends a deal you thought you won.
- Build a named, prioritised list and never walk to a buzzer without a name, a
  signal, a goal, and a plan for the killer.
- AgentBridge's web due-diligence turns an hour of company research into a minute,
  with sources, so you ring warm and specific instead of cold.
