# Growth Hacking: The Buzzer as an Experiment

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Explain what growth hacking is and how it applies to B2B field selling.
- Treat every buzzer visit as a small experiment with a hypothesis and a measure.
- Run a simple A/B test on your own pitch, opening line, or target list.
- Design one growth experiment for your own territory and record the result.

## Key concepts

Growth hacking is a way of working, not a bag of tricks. The core idea is simple:
instead of guessing what will grow your business, you run small, fast, cheap
experiments, measure what happens, keep what works, and drop what does not. It came
from software startups that had no marketing budget and had to find clever,
measurable ways to grow. The method transfers cleanly to the buzzer, because a
cold visit is already a cheap experiment: it costs a pair of shoes and a few
minutes, and you get a clear result — yes, no, or maybe.

### Foundations: growth hacking and B2B performance

A growth experiment has four parts. First a **hypothesis** — a guess you can test,
like "if I name a specific pain in my opening line, more receptionists will pass
me on." Second a **change** — the one thing you vary. Third a **metric** — the
number that tells you if it worked, like the share of visits that become a booked
meeting. Fourth a **decision** — keep the change, or throw it away.

Most sellers run their whole career on one untested script. They say the same
words at every door and wonder why the result never improves. Growth hacking says:
stop guessing. The difference between a seller who improves and one who plateaus
is not talent; it is whether they measure and vary.

In B2B the metrics that matter are few and concrete:

| Metric | What it counts | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Contact rate | Visits that reach a human | Tests your timing and approach |
| Pass-on rate | Visits the gatekeeper routes to a decision maker | Tests your opening line |
| Meeting rate | Visits that become a booked appointment | Your true conversion |
| Qualified rate | Meetings that have real need and budget | Tests your target list |
| Revenue per visit | Money won divided by doors rung | The bottom line of the experiment |

Write these down for every visit. A number you do not record is a lesson you throw
away.

### Every visit is an experiment

Reframe the buzzer in your head. You are not "trying to sell." You are running a
test. When a visit fails, it is not a personal rejection; it is a data point that
says "this version did not work here." That reframe removes the fear and makes
you curious instead of discouraged.

Pick one variable at a time. If you change your opening line, your cards, and your
target list all on the same day, you learn nothing, because you cannot tell which
change moved the number. Change one thing, run it across enough doors to see a
pattern, then change the next thing.

A practical loop:

1. Write your current baseline. Ring twenty doors with your normal script and
   record the meeting rate. Say it is 1 in 20.
2. Form a hypothesis. "Naming a cost the buyer recognises will raise the pass-on
   rate."
3. Change one thing. Add one sentence: "We cut the admin cost that small firms
   pay for manual invoicing."
4. Run twenty doors with the new line. Record again.
5. Compare. If the meeting rate rises, keep the line. If not, drop it and try the
   next idea.

This is the whole method. It is boring, repeatable, and it compounds. A seller who
runs one experiment a week improves their pitch fifty times a year.

### A/B testing and rapid iteration

A/B testing is the formal name for comparing two versions. Version A is your
control (what you always do). Version B is the variant (the change). You show
each version to a similar group of prospects and compare the result. The winner
becomes your new A.

For the buzzer, A/B testing works on almost anything:

- **Opening line.** A: "I'd like to speak to whoever handles office software."
  B: "I help small firms stop losing hours on manual paperwork — who looks after
  that here?"
- **Time of day.** A: mornings. B: late afternoons. Which gets the owner in?
- **The card you leave.** A: a plain card. B: a card with one number ("save 6
  hours a week").
- **The target list.** A: shops. B: workshops. Which has more budget and pain?

Two rules make A/B testing honest. First, change only one variable per test.
Second, run enough attempts to trust the result. Two doors prove nothing; twenty
begin to. If your baseline meeting rate is 5%, you need a large sample before a
difference is real — so keep the test running until the gap is too big to be
luck.

Rapid iteration means you do not wait for a perfect plan. You ship a rough
version, learn, and improve. The first variant is rarely the best. The seller who
tries ten small changes usually finds one that doubles the meeting rate. The
seller who tries to design the perfect pitch in their head finds nothing.

A caution: growth hacking without ethics is manipulation. Every experiment must
stay honest. Never test a lie, a false urgency, or a deceptive claim. The method
is about finding the truest, clearest way to communicate real value — not about
tricking the door open. A grown business built on a deceptive line collapses when
the customer finds out.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Growth hacking | Growing through fast, measured experiments | "Treat the territory like a growth hacker: test, measure, keep." |
| Hypothesis | A testable guess | "My hypothesis: naming a cost raises the pass-on rate." |
| A/B test | Comparing two versions to see which wins | "Run an A/B test on the opening line." |
| Control (A) | The version you always use, kept for comparison | "My normal script is the control." |
| Variant (B) | The changed version you are testing | "Variant B names a specific pain." |
| Metric | The number you measure | "The meeting rate is the metric that matters." |
| Iteration | One round of change-and-learn | "After ten iterations the line was twice as good." |

## A field note

The term "growth hacking" was coined in 2010 by Sean Ellis in a blog post titled
*"Find a Growth Hacker for Your Startup"* (Startup-Marketing.com, 26 June 2010).
He defined a growth hacker as "a person whose true north is growth. Everything
they do is scrutinized by its potential impact on scalable growth." The phrase
was popularised in 2012 by Andrew Chen's post *"Growth Hacker is the new VP
Marketing."* Notice the definition: the growth hacker's loyalty is to the
*metric*, not to the campaign. That is exactly the discipline the buzzer rewards —
the seller who watches the meeting rate, not the one who admires their own script.
(Source: Wikipedia, "Growth hacking.")

## Exercises

1. **Baseline your door.** Ring twenty doors with your current script and record
   the meeting rate. Write the number down. This is your control.
2. **Design one experiment.** Write a hypothesis, pick one variable to change,
   and state the metric. Example: "If I leave a card with a number on it, the
   follow-up reply rate rises." Run twenty doors with the change and compare.
3. **A/B two openers.** Write two opening lines, A and B. Alternate them door to
   door for forty visits and tally the pass-on rate for each. Keep the winner.
4. **Build the sheet.** Make a simple two-column record — control round and
   variant round — and fill it after each round. If you use AgentBridge, ask for
   the KPI dashboard and log both rounds into it.

## Summary

- Growth hacking is growing through small, fast, cheap, measured experiments — a
  method that fits the buzzer perfectly because a cold visit is already cheap and
  gives a clear result.
- Treat every visit as a test: a hypothesis, one changed variable, a metric, and
  a keep-or-drop decision.
- A/B testing compares a control and a variant; change one thing at a time and
  run enough doors to trust the gap.
- Record the numbers in a dashboard so you can see what works and iterate; the
  seller who tests ten small changes usually finds one that doubles the result.
- Keep every experiment honest — the method finds the clearest way to show real
  value, never a way to deceive.
