# Researching the Prospect: Strategic Preparation and Analysis

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Research a company across its website, LinkedIn, news, and financials before you ring.
- Infer a prospect's likely pain points from its sector and its size.
- Analyse the prospect's competitors to sharpen your own angle.
- Build a value hypothesis you can test in the first live conversation.
- Prepare support material and work a pre-call checklist without looking at it.

## Key concepts

The buzzer is the last step of preparation, not the first. A professional rings a
door already knowing who is behind it, what keeps them awake, and what a useful
opening sentence sounds like. The beginner rings blind and lets the conversation
happen to them. The professional researches first, then rings to confirm a
hypothesis, not to discover a stranger.

Preparation is also the cheapest weapon you own. It costs an hour and a browser.
It cannot be taken from you by a bad product or a tight budget. A seller who knows
three true things about a prospect that no competitor bothered to learn has already
won the right to be heard.

### The four sources of company research

Every company leaves a trail. Read it from four places, in this order.

**The website.** This is what they *want* you to see, which is itself useful. Read
the "About" page for how they describe themselves and their customers. Read the
products for what they think matters. Read the news or press section for what they
are proud of. Read the careers page: a company hiring salespeople is growing and
may need your help; a company hiring engineers is building something and may buy
tools. Steal their own words — using a prospect's language back at them signals you
understand their world.

**LinkedIn.** This is the org chart made visible. Find the decision-maker and the
people around them. Note how long each has been in role: a new boss is a chance
(a new boss changes things and buys things), a long-tenured one is a different
pitch. Look at the people who left and why you can guess. Look for shared
connections you can ask for an introduction. A name you can mention — "Dana at
your supplier suggested I call you" — is worth more than any clever line.

**News.** Search the company name plus words like *funding*, *expansion*,
*layoffs*, *acquisition*, *new product*, *lawsuit*, *award*. Each is a hook. A
funding round means money and pressure to grow. Layoffs mean a leaner team doing
more with less. A new product means a launch to support. A regulatory change means
a problem you may solve.

**Financials.** Public companies file annual reports; many countries have public
registries (in the UK, Companies House) where private filings are visible. Look at
the revenue band, the growth rate, the margins, the debt. You do not need to be an
analyst. A company growing fast has different needs than one shrinking. A thin-
margin business cares about cost; a fat-margin one cares about speed and quality.

### Reading pain from sector and size

You cannot research every detail, so use patterns. Sector and size tell you a
great deal before you open a single page.

| Sector | Typical pain | Your angle |
|---|---|---|
| Retail / shops | Thin margins, stock-outs, staff turnover | Save cost, cut waste, train fast |
| Manufacturing | Downtime, quality defects, scheduling | Reduce stoppages, standardise |
| Professional services | Billable hours, lost documents, admin load | Reclaim time, find files instantly |
| Logistics | Routing, delivery failures, tracking | Optimise routes, cut misses |
| Healthcare admin | Paperwork, compliance, waiting times | Automate forms, stay compliant |
| Hospitality | Booking errors, seasonal staff, reviews | Smooth operations, protect reputation |

Size changes the pitch too. A micro business (1–9 people) buys on speed and one
person's gut; the owner *is* the decision. A small business (10–49) has a named
buyer and cares about payback. A mid-size firm (50–250) has a process, a budget
cycle, and more than one person to convince. Match your depth to their size: a
micro owner wants a one-line promise; a mid-size buyer wants a business case.

### Analysing the competitor set

Know who the prospect might choose instead of you. Find their competitors and ask
three questions: what do they charge, what do they claim, and where do they fall
short? The gap between a competitor's promise and the prospect's real problem is
your opening. If a rival is cheap but slow, you sell reliability. If a rival is
big and generic, you sell fit and service. Never trash a competitor by name;
simply know the ground so you can stand on the higher part of it.

### The value hypothesis

Research ends in one sentence you can hold in your head at the buzzer:

> "Because [their situation], they likely feel [the pain], and if [what we do]
> can [the outcome], that is worth [the value] to them."

This is a hypothesis, not a fact. You ring to test it. If the first answer confirms
it, push deeper. If it breaks, you have learned something and can pivot on the
spot. A seller without a hypothesis has nothing to test and drifts.

### Preparing support material

Bring or be ready to send one thing that makes the abstract concrete: a one-page
summary, a short case study of a similar company, a sample of the output, a small
deck. The material is not the pitch; it is the proof you can leave behind so the
conversation continues after you go. Prepare it before you ring, not after they
say yes.

### The pre-call checklist

Before you walk to any buzzer, know these. If you cannot fill a line, that is your
research task.

| What to know | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The company's real business | So you never misdescribe them |
| The decision-maker's name and role | So you ask for the right person |
| One true recent fact about them | So you open with relevance |
| Their likely top pain | So your first line lands |
| One competitor and its weakness | So you know your ground |
| Your value hypothesis | So you have something to test |
| The thing you will leave behind | So the sale outlives the visit |

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-call research | Work done before the visit | "The pre-call took an hour and won the meeting." |
| Pain point | A specific problem the buyer feels | "Their top pain point is staff turnover." |
| Value hypothesis | A testable guess at your worth to them | "My hypothesis: faster quotes win orders." |
| Decision-maker | The person who can say yes | "LinkedIn showed the owner decides alone." |
| Competitor set | The rivals they might choose | "Our competitor set is two big generic tools." |
| Support material | Proof you can leave behind | "I left a one-page case study." |

**Sales angle:** the seller who arrives knowing the competitor's price and the
prospect's own recent news sounds like an insider, not a stranger. Research is
what turns "another salesperson" into "this person did their homework," and that
is the difference between being buzzed through and being buzzed off.

## A field note

Long before the internet made research easy, one man made preparation a profession.
In the 1880s John Henry Patterson took over the failing National Cash Register
Company in Dayton, Ohio, and built what is often called the world's first sales
training school, on the factory grounds at a site he nicknamed "Sugar Camp." New
sellers were drilled from a short handbook rather than left to improvise, and the
discipline spread: between roughly 1910 and 1930 it was estimated that one in six
American business executives had passed through NCR. Patterson turned selling
from a haphazard itinerant trade into a trained, prepared craft — the ancestor of
every pre-call checklist a buzzer seller uses today. (Source: Wikipedia, "John
Henry Patterson (NCR owner).")

## Exercises

1. **Four-source pass.** Pick one real prospect and research it across website,
   LinkedIn, news, and financials in 30 minutes. Write one line from each source.
2. **Pain map.** From the sector table, list the three most likely pains for your
   next target, then rank them by how much each probably costs them per month.
3. **Hypothesis drill.** Write your value hypothesis as one sentence for three
   prospects. Read each aloud at the door and see whether the first answer confirms
   or breaks it.
4. **Leave-behind test.** For one prospect, prepare a single page you could hand
   over that proves your point. If you cannot make it fit on one page, your
   thinking is not clear yet.

## Summary

- The buzzer is the last step of preparation; research who is behind it before you
  ring, not after.
- Read the company from four sources — website, LinkedIn, news, financials — and
  steal their own language.
- Sector and size predict pain and pitch: match your depth to their size and lead
  with their most expensive problem.
- Know the competitor set and stand on the higher ground; end research in one
  testable value hypothesis.
- AgentBridge researches competitors and market trends and answers from your own
  documents in plain sentences, so you arrive prepared to be mistaken for an
  insider.
