# The Buzzer in B2B: What It Means to Sell Cold in a Company

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Say plainly what "door-to-door B2B" means and how it differs from cold calling,
  telemarketing, and field selling.
- Explain why a physical, uninvited visit still works in a digital market — and
  where its real limits are.
- Trace the B2B sales cycle from the first buzzer to a signed contract.
- Name the common myths about cold selling and answer them with facts.

## Key concepts

Selling "cold" simply means selling to someone who has not asked you to come. You
have no appointment, no warm introduction, no prior relationship. The buzzer is
the purest form of this: you arrive at a company you have never met and ask, in
person, to speak to someone who can help. Everything in this book builds from that
uncomfortable, honest starting point.

### What we mean by door-to-door B2B

Door-to-door B2B is field selling aimed at businesses, not homes. You walk into a
company's premises — a workshop, an office, a warehouse, a shop's back entrance —
and try to start a conversation with a decision maker. The "door" is not a house
door; it is the reception desk, the intercom, the gate. The target is not a family
but an organisation with budgets, roles, and a purchasing process.

This matters because the business buyer is different from the consumer. A business
buys to solve a problem that costs it money or time. It buys with more than one
person involved. It buys on logic first and emotion second, and it remembers the
purchase for years. A door-to-door B2B visit is therefore not a pitch; it is the
opening of a process that may run for weeks or months. Your job at the buzzer is
not to sell the product. It is to earn the next step.

### The different kinds of "cold"

People mix up several selling modes. They overlap, but they are not the same. The
table below sorts them.

| Mode | Where it happens | Target | What "cold" means here | Typical goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Door-to-door B2B | In person, at the company | Businesses | No appointment, no prior contact | Book a meeting / demo |
| B2C selling | In person or shop | Consumers | No prior relationship | Immediate or quick sale |
| Cold calling | Phone, dialled by you | Either | No prior contact, you dialled | Get interest, book a call |
| Telemarketing | Phone, scripted, high volume | Mostly consumers | Mass outbound calls | Lead capture / direct sale |
| Field selling | On the road, scheduled or walk-in | Businesses | Visits away from your office | Cover accounts, open new ones |

Two points to fix the differences in your mind. First, B2C (business-to-consumer)
selling targets individuals buying for themselves; the decision is fast and
personal. B2B (business-to-business) targets organisations; the decision is slow
and shared. Second, cold calling and telemarketing happen on the phone, where you
can be hung up on in three seconds and never seen. The buzzer happens in person,
where you cannot be hung up on, and where a human face and a calm voice do work a
phone line cannot. Field selling is the wider category — any selling done out in
the territory — and door-to-door B2B is its most direct, most courageous form.

### Why the buzzer still works

The obvious objection is that nobody wants a stranger at the door. The evidence
says otherwise, if you understand why it works.

The buzzer works because it is rare. In a world of email and ads, a real person
appearing in the lobby cannot be filtered, muted, or scrolled past. It forces a
small act of human attention. Rarity is value: when almost no one rings, the one
who does is memorable.

It works because presence builds trust faster than any remote channel. A buyer can
see that you are real, that you are calm, that you showed up and meant it. Trust is
the scarce commodity in selling, and physical presence is the fastest way to lay a
little of it down.

It works because it is cheap to start. A phone campaign needs lists, diallers, and
budget. A buzzer needs a pair of shoes and the nerve to walk in. Your cost per
attempt is almost zero, so even a low hit rate can pay.

It works because it reaches the people who never answer the phone. The owner of a
small firm may ignore unknown numbers all day but cannot ignore a polite person
standing in front of him.

But know the limits. The buzzer is low-yield per visit: you will hear "not
interested" far more than "tell me more." It is time-expensive — you can only ring
so many doors a day. It suits products that can be shown quickly and priced for a
small or medium business. It is harder in high-security buildings, in cultures that
dislike unannounced visits, and for very large enterprise deals where the decision
never happens at a reception desk. The buzzer opens doors; it does not, by itself,
close enterprise contracts. Treat it as the first move of a longer game, not the
whole game.

And name the prejudices so they lose their power. "It's outdated" — it is older
than email and still works, which is the opposite of outdated. "It's pushy" — a
polite visit that accepts a no is not pushy; a deceptive one is. "Nobody buys like
that anymore" — people buy in person every day; they just do not see the sellers
who do it well, because they are too busy working to post about it online.

### The B2B sales cycle: from buzzer to contract

A cold visit is step one of a longer cycle. Knowing the whole cycle keeps you from
trying to cram a contract into thirty seconds at a desk.

| Stage | What happens | Your job at the buzzer |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Contact | You ring, you speak to someone | Be present, be clear, survive the gatekeeper |
| 2. Qualify | Find out if they have the need and budget | Ask one or two sharp questions |
| 3. Appointment | Book a real demo or meeting | Get a date, a time, a named person |
| 4. Discovery | Understand their problem deeply | (Later visit) listen more than talk |
| 5. Proposal | Show the solution and the price | (Later) match value to their pain |
| 6. Negotiation | Agree terms | (Later) trade, do not just cut |
| 7. Close | Sign | (Later) ask clearly for the yes |
| 8. Delivery / after-sales | Deliver, support, renew | (Later) be reliable, ask for referrals |

At the buzzer you are only in stages 1 to 3. Your single goal is to turn a stranger
into a booked appointment with a named person. Do not try to sell the product,
quote a price, or handle deep objections at the desk. Earn the meeting; the meeting
does the rest.

### Myths to bust

- **"You have to close at the door."** No. You close the *appointment*. Trying to
  close a sale at a reception desk feels like pressure and rarely works.
- **"The gatekeeper is the enemy."** No. She is a filter you pass by being honest
  and useful, not by tricking her.
- **"A no means never."** No. A no today is often a "not now." Follow-up turns
  many nos into yeses later.
- **"Only extroverts can do this."** No. The buzzer rewards preparation and
  calm, not loudness. Many quiet sellers are excellent at it.
- **"It's dead because of the internet."** No. The internet made digital contact
  cheap and crowded, which made rare human contact more valuable, not less.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Cold contact | No prior relationship or appointment | "This buzzer is cold — they've never heard of us." |
| Gatekeeper | The person who controls access to the decision maker | "The receptionist is the gatekeeper; be honest with her." |
| Decision maker | The person who can say yes and sign | "Ask to speak to the decision maker on office software." |
| Qualify | Check whether the prospect is worth pursuing | "Qualify before you pitch: do they have the need and the budget?" |
| Appointment | A booked meeting with a named person | "The buzzer's only goal is the appointment." |
| Hit rate | How many attempts turn into a result | "My hit rate was two meetings from twenty doors." |

## AgentBridge in action

The hardest part of a cold visit is the first thirty seconds: you have to sound
approachable, not like a script. AgentBridge is built around exactly that quality —
a first conversation that feels like talking to a person, not operating software.

The real first-chat example from the product is a single plain sentence a new user
types on day one:

> Hello! What can you help me with in my office?

The agent answers in plain words and lists what it can do — write documents, build
spreadsheets, read and send email, research the web, make presentations — and the
user simply keeps talking to it like a person. Everything happens in one window,
with F1 for help and a bottom bar that always shows what is active.

**Sales angle:** when you demo this at a company, you are not showing a tool that
needs training. You are showing a tool whose very first interaction is a normal
conversation. That is the same approachability you used at the buzzer, now inside
the product. The buyer thinks: "if it's this easy to start, my staff will actually
use it." The door and the demo share one quality — being easy to talk to — and that
consistency is what wins the meeting.

## A field note

The door-to-door tradition has a long, respectable history. The Fuller Brush
Company, founded in 1906 by Alfred Fuller in Hartford, Connecticut, built a
national brand on men who walked up to doors and asked to show their brushes.
Fuller later titled his 1960 autobiography *A Foot in the Door* — a phrase drawn
straight from the salesman's habit of putting a foot in the doorway to keep a
conversation going long enough to turn it into a sale. The image is crude, but the
lesson is real: the whole trade lives in holding the door open just long enough to
be useful. Today the foot is replaced by a reason — a clear, honest offer of value
that makes the buyer want to keep the door open themselves. (Source: Wikipedia,
"Fuller Brush Company.")

## Exercises

1. **Write your buzzer line.** In one breath, write what you would say to a
   receptionist: who you are, what you do, and the one-line reason they should
   pass you on. Read it aloud ten times until it is smooth and under fifteen words.
2. **Map the cycle.** Take a product you know and write, for each of the eight
   stages above, the single next action you would take. Notice how little of the
   list happens at the buzzer itself.
3. **Answer the myths.** For each myth in the last section, write a one-sentence
   rebuttal you could say to a colleague who believes it. If you cannot rebut it,
   you have found a real weakness to think about.
4. **Rehearse the no.** Have a partner say "not interested" five times in a row.
   Practise replying politely, leaving a card, and walking out calm. The goal is to
   make a no feel ordinary, not fatal.

## Summary

- Door-to-door B2B is the direct, in-person form of cold selling: you arrive at a
  business with no appointment and try to start a conversation.
- It differs from B2C (slower, shared decisions), cold calling and telemarketing
  (phone, easily hung up on), and general field selling (the wider category).
- The buzzer still works because it is rare, builds trust through presence, is
  cheap to start, and reaches people who never answer the phone — but it is
  low-yield per visit and suits quick, demonstrable products for small and medium
  firms.
- At the door your only job is stages 1 to 3: contact, qualify, book the
  appointment. The meeting, not the buzzer, closes the contract.
- Most fears about cold selling are myths; the real skill is preparation, honesty,
  and calm.
