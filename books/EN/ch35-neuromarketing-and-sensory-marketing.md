# Neuromarketing and Sensory Marketing

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Explain what neuromarketing is and what it can and cannot tell a seller.
- Describe sensory marketing in a B2B setting, not just a shop.
- Control the sensory signals you send at the buzzer — voice, face, touch, space.
- Run a simple sensory audit of your own presence.

## Key concepts

Neuromarketing is the study of how the brain responds to marketing — what grabs
attention, what builds trust, what triggers a "yes" before the person can name
why. Sensory marketing is its practical cousin: the deliberate use of sight,
sound, touch, smell, and space to shape how someone feels about you. In B2B we
like to pretend buying is pure logic. It is not. The buyer is a person, and a
person's senses decide how safe you feel long before the spreadsheet opens.

### Foundations: neuroscience in B2B

The brain makes fast, quiet judgements. Before you finish your first sentence,
the buyer has already read your face, your posture, your tone, and filed you as
safe or unsafe, calm or pushy. Neuromarketing studies these quick judgements. It
does not give you a magic button; it gives you a warning that you are being
judged on more than your words, and a map of which signals matter.

Two findings are worth holding onto. First, **threat shuts down buying.** A brain
that senses danger — a hard sell, a too-close stance, a rushed pitch — moves into
defence and stops listening. Much of selling is not adding desire; it is removing
threat. Second, **trust is sensory.** People judge trustworthiness in a fraction
of a second from a calm face, a steady voice, and unhurried body language. You
can be right about the product and still lose because you looked nervous or came
on too strong.

A caution about the field: neuromarketing is real but often oversold. Brain scans
tell researchers how groups respond to ads; they do not let you read one
person's mind at a door. Treat the insights as common sense made sharper, not as
a secret weapon. The honest version of neuromarketing is simply paying attention
to how a human being experiences you.

### Sensory marketing in B2B

Sensory marketing is usually sold as shop design — the smell in a hotel lobby,
the music in a café. In B2B it is subtler but no less real. The "sensory
product" is you and the moment you create.

| Sense | B2B channel | What it signals |
|---|---|---|
| Sight | Your face, dress, posture, the card | Calm, tidy, professional |
| Sound | Your voice, pace, tone | Steady, warm, in control |
| Touch | The handshake, the card, the product sample | Solid, real, considered |
| Space | Distance, where you stand, eye level | Respect, confidence, no threat |
| Time | How long you take, whether you rush | You have nothing to hide |

The seller who understands this tunes each channel. A voice that drops slightly
at the end of a sentence sounds calmer and more certain than one that rises. A
pause before answering signals thought, not hesitation. Standing at a slight
angle rather than square-on feels less confrontational. A card with some weight
to it feels more substantial than a flimsy slip. These are small things, and
small things are what the senses actually notice.

### The seller's sensory impact

You are the medium. Everything the buyer senses about you colours whether they
believe you. Work the obvious ones first.

**Voice.** Slow down. Nervous sellers speed up. A slower pace reads as authority
and calm. Lower your pitch a touch at the end of key lines. Breathe before you
speak, not mid-sentence.

**Face and eyes.** A relaxed face and steady (not staring) eye contact say you
are safe. A forced smile reads as false. Aim for calm interest, not performance.

**Body.** Stand open, hands visible, at a respectful distance. Do not crowd the
desk or block the exit — both trigger a threat response. Mirror the buyer's
energy gently; if they are brisk, be brisk; if they settle, you settle.

**Objects.** The card, the sample, the tablet you show — make them feel solid and
clean. A fumbled, crumpled card undercuts a confident voice.

The goal across all of this is one feeling: **safe, calm, in no rush.** A buyer
who feels safe leans in. A buyer who feels rushed or crowded puts their guard up
and looks for the door. Your sensory presence sets that feeling before your words
ever reach the argument.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Neuromarketing | Study of how the brain responds to marketing | "Neuromarketing says the buyer judges you before you finish talking." |
| Sensory marketing | Shaping feeling through the five senses | "Your voice and posture are sensory marketing." |
| Threat response | The brain's defence that blocks buying | "A rushed pitch triggers a threat response." |
| Sensory footprint | The total sensory impression you leave | "Your voice, card and space form your sensory footprint." |
| Trust signal | A cue that reads as trustworthy | "A calm, steady voice is a trust signal." |

## AgentBridge in action

A seller's sensory footprint is no longer only physical. The way your assistant
sounds and reaches a customer is part of the impression too — and AgentBridge
gives you a consistent, calm presence across channels: voice on the phone, chat
in Telegram, a browser window, all the same assistant, all reachable.

The real example is a phone call to the assistant:

> (phone) What did I agree to deliver to the cafe this week?

You call in, say what you need, and the agent answers by voice from your notes —
protected by a PIN so only you can reach it. The same assistant answers in a
Telegram chat, reads a summary aloud, and takes a dictated note. For a customer,
this means your "presence" is always calm, always on, never flustered: a
dependable voice and a quick reply, whatever channel they reach for.

**Sales angle:** reliability is a sensory signal too. A seller whose follow-up is
instant and whose answers are ready feels solid and safe to buy from. The
multi-channel presence becomes part of the trust you build — the same calm you
projected at the door is still there when the buyer calls at nine at night.

## A field note

The term "neuromarketing" surfaced in 2002 — in the master's thesis of Philippe
Morel, in work by the Atlanta firm BrightHouse (which used fMRI brain scans for
market research), and with the Dutch professor Ale Smidts. The field grew out of
older work: Gerald Zaltman patented the Zaltman Metaphor Elicitation Technique
in the 1990s and began using fMRI in 1999 to link brain activity to marketing
stimuli. A 2004 fMRI study of the "Pepsi Challenge" blind taste test brought the
idea to wide public attention, showing that knowing which drink you were tasting
changed how the brain responded. The lesson for sellers is old and simple:
context and feeling shape the experience of the thing itself. (Source:
Wikipedia, "Neuromarketing.")

## Exercises

1. **Record yourself.** Record your opening line on a phone. Listen only to the
   pace and pitch. Slow it down and lower it slightly at the end. Replay until it
   sounds calm and certain.
2. **Space drill.** Practise your approach with a partner. Note the distance at
   which they lean in versus step back. Find the distance that feels respectful,
   not crowding.
3. **Object check.** Pick up the card and sample you carry. Do they feel solid
   and clean, or flimsy? Replace or tidy anything that undercuts your voice.
4. **Sensory audit.** Before your next visit, write one line for each sense —
   what they will see, hear, touch, and how you will hold space. Fix the weakest
   one before you go.

## Summary

- Neuromarketing studies the brain's fast, quiet judgements; in selling it means
  recognising you are judged on more than your words.
- Threat shuts down buying and trust is sensory — much of the job is removing
  threat, not adding pressure.
- In B2B the sensory product is you: voice, face, body, objects, and space all
  signal whether you are safe and calm.
- Tune the channels: slow voice, open body, solid objects, respectful distance,
  no rush.
- Your sensory footprint now includes your digital presence — a calm, always-on,
  reliable assistant extends the trust you set at the door.
