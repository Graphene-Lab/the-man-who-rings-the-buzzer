# The Seller's Vocabulary: An Essential Glossary

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Define and use the core selling terms in plain words, grouped by what they are for.
- Tell the difference between terms people confuse (lead vs prospect, value
  proposition vs USP, ROI vs TCO).
- Use the persuasion and negotiation vocabulary correctly in a real sentence.
- Recognise how culture changes the meaning of some terms across markets.

## Key concepts

A trade that cannot name its own tools cannot teach them. Selling has a working
vocabulary, and once you own it, conversations with buyers, managers, and
colleagues get sharper and faster. This glossary is grouped by purpose: the basics
you use every day, the preparation terms you use before a visit, the psychology
terms behind why people say yes, the negotiation terms for the bargaining table,
and the cultural terms that change meaning across borders.

Each term has a plain one-line definition and a short sentence showing how it is
used. Learn them so well that you could explain each to a smart stranger in one
breath. A word you cannot define is a word you should not use at a door.

### The basics

These are the words you will meet in every sales conversation and every report.

| Term | Plain definition | Usage sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Lead | A person or company that might buy, but is not yet qualified | "The trade-show list gave us forty new leads." |
| Prospect | A lead you have checked and judged worth pursuing | "After the call, three leads became real prospects." |
| Gatekeeper | The person who controls access to the decision maker | "Be honest with the gatekeeper; she decides if you get in." |
| Decision maker | The person who can say yes and sign | "Ask to speak to the decision maker on office software." |
| Buyer persona | A profile of your ideal buyer, built from real traits | "Our buyer persona is a 45-year-old operations owner." |
| Pipeline | The list of all open deals, by stage | "My pipeline has twelve deals between contact and close." |
| CRM | Software that records every contact and deal (Customer Relationship Management) | "Log the visit in the CRM before you forget it." |
| KPI | A number you track to measure performance (Key Performance Indicator) | "Doors rung and meetings booked are my two KPIs." |
| Conversion rate | The share of attempts that reach the next step | "Two meetings from twenty doors is a 10% conversion rate." |

A lead is raw; a prospect is checked. The pipeline is the whole funnel of open
deals; the CRM is the tool that holds it. A KPI is any number you watch; the
conversion rate is the KPI that tells you how efficiently you move people forward.

### Preparation

You use these before you ring, to decide who to ring and what to say.

| Term | Plain definition | Usage sentence |
|---|---|---|
| ICP | The type of company that fits you best (Ideal Customer Profile) | "A 10-to-50-person office with admin pain is our ICP." |
| Lead scoring | Ranking leads by how likely they are to buy | "We score leads by size, need, and budget." |
| Pain point | The specific problem that costs the buyer money or time | "Their pain point is hours lost re-typing invoices." |
| Value proposition | The clear promise of benefit you offer | "Our value proposition: automate the office, keep your data." |
| USP | What makes you different from every rival (Unique Selling Proposition) | "Our USP is that nothing leaves your own machine." |
| ROI | What you get back compared to what you spend (Return on Investment) | "The ROI is positive in four months." |
| TCO | The full cost of owning something over time (Total Cost of Ownership) | "The TCO includes licences, upkeep, and training, not just the price." |

The ICP says who to chase; lead scoring says who to chase first. The pain point is
the buyer's wound; the value proposition is your promise to heal it; the USP is why
your promise beats the rival's. ROI measures the gain; TCO measures the whole cost.
A buyer who trusts you will ask for both, so have both ready.

### Psychology

These are the mental forces that make a person say yes. They are real, and they are
not tricks — they work only when the offer is honest.

| Term | Plain definition | Usage sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Rapport | A feeling of easy trust and connection | "Small talk builds rapport before business talk." |
| Social proof | People follow what others like them have done | "Say three firms in their town already use it." |
| Authority | People trust those who seem to know | "A cited report gives you instant authority." |
| Scarcity | People want what is limited or running out | "Only ten onboarding slots this month" uses scarcity. |
| Reciprocity | People feel obliged to return a favour | "A free useful report triggers reciprocity." |
| Commitment | A small yes makes a bigger yes likelier | "Getting them to try a demo is a commitment step." |
| Consistency | People act to match what they already said they are | "If they say they value privacy, sell the local model." |

Rapport opens the door. Social proof and authority make the choice feel safe.
Scarcity and reciprocity add push. Commitment and consistency keep a person moving
in a direction they have already started. Use them to be clear, never to deceive —
a scarcity that is a lie is a trust you cannot buy back.

### Negotiation

These belong at the bargaining table, after the value is understood.

| Term | Plain definition | Usage sentence |
|---|---|---|
| BATNA | Your best option if you walk away from the deal (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) | "Know your BATNA before you lower your price." |
| ZOPA | The range where a deal is possible for both sides (Zone of Possible Agreement) | "If their ceiling is above your floor, you have a ZOPA." |
| Closing | Asking clearly for the agreement | "Closing is a clear request, not a trap." |
| Upselling | Selling a higher version of what they want | "Upselling means the bigger plan that fits them better." |
| Cross-selling | Selling an extra, related product | "Cross-selling adds the scheduling module to the deal." |

BATNA is your safety net; a strong BATNA lets you say no and negotiate calmly.
ZOPA is the overlap where both sides can win — no overlap, no deal. Closing is the
honest ask. Upselling goes up the same ladder; cross-selling adds a new ladder
beside it.

### Cultural

The same word can mean different things across markets. These terms help you read
the room when you cross a border.

| Term | Plain definition | Usage sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Power distance | How much a culture accepts unequal rank and authority | "In high power-distance cultures, go to the boss, not the clerk." |
| High-context / low-context | How much meaning is left unsaid vs stated plainly | "Japan is high-context; Germany is low-context and direct." |
| Guanxi | In China, the network of trusted relationships that makes business possible | "In China you build guanxi before you sign anything." |
| Nemawashi | In Japan, the quiet behind-the-scenes consensus-building before a decision | "The real meeting happens in nemawashi, not the boardroom." |

Power distance tells you who must be in the room. High- versus low-context tells
you whether to say things plainly or read between the lines. Guanxi and nemawashi
both say the same thing in two cultures: the deal is made by relationships built
over time, not by a single pitch at a desk. A buzzer that works in a low-context,
low-power-distance market may need patience and introductions to work in a
high-context one.

## Terminology in practice

A glossary is dead until the words move. Here is the same vocabulary used live,
through two real AgentBridge actions that a seller can demo in seconds.

The first is turning tools on. The seller types `/tools` and a checklist appears;
they tick the tools the buyer cares about — say, the spreadsheet tool — and the
choice is remembered for next time. Watch the terms come alive: the buyer's *pain
point* (hours lost on spreadsheets) meets the *value proposition* (turn one tool
on, the work is done), and the *USP* is that it all runs on their own machine.

The second is switching the answering AI. The seller types `/model` and picks a
different AI — a local one that keeps data on the machine, or a cloud one that is
usually sharper — and the conversation continues unchanged. The *documents, tools,
and history stay exactly where they are; only the answering AI changes.*

| Term | How the demo shows it |
|---|---|
| Pain point | "You lose hours on spreadsheets" — the buyer's real wound. |
| Value proposition | "Tick one tool and the spreadsheet is built for you." |
| USP | "Switch to the local model and nothing leaves your machine." |
| Consistency | The conversation and history stay the same across every switch. |
| ROI | "The time you save pays for the tool in a few months." |

The point of the demo is not the buttons. It is that a seller who knows the words
can narrate the buttons as value. The same two commands, described without
vocabulary, are a tech trick. Described with it, they are a sales story.

## A field note

Some selling words are young and precise. "BATNA" — the Best Alternative to a
Negotiated Agreement, the thing you can fall back on if you walk away — was
popularised by Roger Fisher and William Ury in their 1981 book *Getting to Yes:
Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*. In it they defined BATNA as "the results
you can obtain without negotiating." The idea reframed negotiation: your power at
the table comes not from being louder, but from having a good option outside it.
A seller who knows their BATNA can hold a price without anger, because they are
never truly desperate. (Source: Wikipedia, "Getting to Yes.")

## Exercises

1. **Define in one breath.** Cover the right-hand column and, for each term, say
   its plain definition out loud in one sentence. Flag any you stumble on and
   re-learn those first.
2. **Pair the confusions.** Write three pairs you often mix up (lead/prospect,
   value proposition/USP, ROI/TCO) and one line on how each pair differs.
3. **Narrate a demo.** Take any product you sell and describe one feature using
   five terms from the psychology group in a single short paragraph.
4. **Cross the border.** Pick one cultural term and write how the same buzzer line
   would change in a market that scores high on it.

## Summary

- Selling's vocabulary is grouped by purpose: basics, preparation, psychology,
  negotiation, and culture.
- Confusable pairs have clean lines: a lead is raw, a prospect is checked; a value
  proposition is your promise, a USP is why you beat the rival; ROI is the gain,
  TCO is the whole cost.
- Persuasion terms (rapport, social proof, authority, scarcity, reciprocity,
  commitment, consistency) describe why people say yes — and only work on an
  honest offer.
- Negotiation terms (BATNA, ZOPA, closing, upselling, cross-selling) belong at
  the table; a strong BATNA is your real power.
- Cultural terms (power distance, high/low-context, guanxi, nemawashi) change how
  the same words land across markets.
