# Surrounding Information: Clothing, Context, and Timing

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Use clothing and grooming to signal that you belong in the buyer's world.
- Read and shape the physical context of a visit so the setting works for you.
- Choose the timing — hour, day, and moment in the buyer's cycle — that raises your odds.
- Control the first minute with nonverbal immediacy so the frame is set before you speak.
- Run a short self-presentation checklist before every visit.

## Key concepts

### You are read before you are heard

Long before your first sentence, the buyer has taken in a bundle of information around you: what you wear, where you are standing, what time it is, how you carried yourself through the door. None of it is your pitch, and all of it colours the pitch. Call it **surrounding information** — the message made of everything except your words. Most sellers spend their preparation on the words and nothing on the surround. That is backwards. The surround sets whether the words get a fair hearing.

### Clothing and appearance

Clothing is the loudest piece of surrounding information. It is read in a fraction of a second and it answers a question the buyer never asks out loud: *is this person for me?* The rule is not "dress to impress." It is **dress to fit the buyer's world, one notch up**. You want to look like you belong in their company and care a little more than they do — never like you are trying to outshine them or sell them a costume.

That means the right clothes are different for every door. A machine shop, a law firm and a design studio are three different dress codes, and the same suit that reads "professional" at one reads "out of place" at another. Learn the buyer's world before you dress for it. Then the non-negotiables that hold everywhere: clothes that **fit** (the single biggest tell — a bad fit signals a person who does not notice detail), clean and pressed, shoes that are not scuffed, grooming tidy, and nothing that draws the eye away from your face — no loud tie, no jangling jewellery, no strong cologne that fills the room before you do.

### Enclothed cognition: the clothes change you too

Clothing does not only change how they see you. It changes how you think and act. Psychologists Adam and Galinsky named this **enclothed cognition** in 2012: the effect of what you wear on your own mind. In their study, people told they were wearing a doctor's white coat paid noticeably better attention on a detail task than people in their own clothes — and the effect needed both the symbolic meaning of the coat *and* actually wearing it. A coat described as a painter's coat did not help.

You do not need to believe every detail of that study to use the principle (the honest caveats are in the field note below). The practical version is simple: when you dress the part, you tend to act the part. Put on clothes that mean "capable professional" to you and you stand straighter, speak with more conviction, and hold the frame better. Dress for the person you are trying to be in the room, and the room tends to meet you there.

### The physical context

Where a conversation happens shapes what can happen in it. A noisy factory floor, a busy reception, a café, a boardroom, a car park — each carries its own constraints and its own status. Before you arrive, think about the setting as something you can read and partly choose.

Three things to manage. **Noise and interruption**: a loud or busy place kills a delicate conversation; if you must talk there, keep it short and concrete, save the nuance for a quieter channel. **Seating and status**: sitting across a big desk is a negotiation frame; sitting side by side, or both standing at a sample, is a collaboration frame; standing while they sit puts you below them. Choose the frame that fits the ask. **Control of the space**: in their office they have home advantage — they know where the exits are, they can be interrupted on purpose. Your job is not to win the territory but to not lose the moment to it: arrive on time, claim your seat calmly, and do not let a busy setting rush you off your own points.

### Timing

Timing is the surrounding information that sellers leave to chance most often, and it is worth a great deal. Three layers.

**Hour and day.** Avoid the Monday-morning flood and the Friday-late drift, when attention is elsewhere. Mid-morning and mid-afternoon on a midweek day are usually calmer. Learn your specific buyer's rhythm rather than trusting a generic rule.

**The moment in their cycle.** A buyer who has just closed a big deal, just survived a crisis, or is about to face a deadline is a different buyer from one in a quiet week. Aim for the moment when your offer solves a problem they are actively feeling. Catching a manager right after a stock-out, or right after a lost customer, is worth more than the same pitch in a calm month.

**Your own readiness.** The right moment is also the moment you are prepared. A call made at nine with the numbers already in your hand beats the same call at eleven made cold. Timing is not only luck — you can arrange the conditions that let you pick the right moment. That is exactly what the AgentBridge box below is about.

### The first minute: nonverbal immediacy

The first sixty seconds set the frame for everything after. Psychologists use the term **immediacy** for the behaviours that close the distance and signal warmth and availability: approaching, smiling, the handshake, eye contact, an open posture, a slight lean in. These are small and they are not subtle, and they decide whether the buyer leans toward you or braces against you before you have said anything of substance.

Run the first minute deliberately. Arrive on time and unhurried. Meet the eyes and smile before the words. Offer a firm, warm handshake. Keep your arms open and your body turned toward them. Lean in slightly when they speak. Say their name. None of this is technique for its own sake; it is the physical signal that says "I am glad to be here and I am glad you are here," which is the one thing the first minute must communicate. A rushed, closed, distracted first minute puts the whole meeting on the defensive.

### A checklist for self-presentation before the visit

Run this in the car or the lift. It takes two minutes and it covers the whole surround.

| Check | The question you answer |
|-------|-----------------------|
| Clothing | Do I fit this buyer's world, one notch up, and does everything fit me? |
| Grooming | Hair, hands, shoes, breath — nothing that pulls the eye or the nose? |
| Context | Where are we meeting, and what frame (seated, standing, side by side) do I want? |
| Timing | Is this the right hour, the right day, the right moment in their cycle? |
| Readiness | Do I have my facts settled so I am not scrambling at the door? |
| First minute | Have I decided my approach, smile, handshake, eyes and opening line? |
| State | Am I calm and unhurried, or am I carrying the last door into this one? |

## A field note

The term **enclothed cognition** was coined by Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky in a 2012 paper in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* (48:4, pp. 918–925). In one experiment, students told they were wearing a doctor's white coat made about half as many errors on a selective-attention task as those in their own clothes. In another, the boost appeared only when the coat carried a "doctor" meaning *and* was actually worn — being told it was a painter's coat, or merely seeing it on the desk, did not help. The honest footnote matters: a later preregistered replication (Burns and colleagues, 2019, same journal) failed to reproduce the Stroop result, and a 2023 meta-analysis by Horton, Adam and Galinsky found the earliest studies weak on replicability, with the evidence firming up only in later work. The takeaway a seller can safely keep is the principle, not the headline number: what you wear can nudge both your own mind and the buyer's read of you — a nudge, not a magic coat. (Sources: Adam & Galinsky, 2012, *JESP*; Burns et al., 2019, *JESP*; Horton, Adam & Galinsky, 2023, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*.)

## Exercises

1. **The dress audit.** Pick your three most common buyer types. For each, lay out the outfit you would wear and check it against one rule: does it say "I belong here, and I care a little more"? Photograph each outfit and look at them as a stranger would. Fix the fit, the shoes, and anything that pulls the eye from your face.
2. **The context walk.** Before your next visit, arrive ten minutes early and study the meeting place. Decide in advance the frame you want — seated across, side by side, or standing at a sample — and where you will position yourself. Note the noise, the interruptions and the exits. Walk in with the setting already planned, not discovered.
3. **The timing map.** For one real buyer, sketch their week: the morning flood, the lunch drift, the Friday fade, and the moments in their business cycle when your offer solves a live problem. Mark the one window that is best. Plan your next contact inside it, and schedule the information you need beforehand so you are ready when it opens.
4. **The first-minute rehearsal.** With a partner, drill only the first sixty seconds: approach, eye contact, smile, handshake, name, opening line, a lean in. Run it five times. After each, have your partner rate one thing only — did the first minute make them lean toward you or brace against you? Repeat until the answer is always "toward."

## Summary

- Everything except your words — clothing, setting, timing, the first minute — is surrounding information that decides whether your words get a fair hearing.
- Dress to fit the buyer's world one notch up, with fit and grooming as the non-negotiables; the clothes also change your own mind (enclothed cognition).
- Read and choose the physical context and the frame it implies, and pick the hour, day and moment in the buyer's cycle that raise your odds.
- Own the first minute with immediacy — approach, smile, handshake, eyes, open posture — because it fixes the frame before you speak.
- Run the two-minute pre-visit checklist, and use the agent's scheduling so you always walk in informed and on time.
