# Conversion and Pipeline Analysis

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Run a win/loss analysis and turn vague "why" answers into tracked categories.
- Find the bottleneck stage in your pipeline from conversion numbers.
- Run a continuous improvement loop: measure, diagnose, change one thing, re-measure.
- Treat every script as a hypothesis and run a simple A/B test on your openers.
- Write a controlled experiment protocol for your own cold outreach.

## Key concepts

### From counting to diagnosing

The KPI chapter taught you to count. This chapter teaches you to **diagnose**. Counting answers "how did we do?"; analysis answers "why, and what do we change Monday morning?" A seller with numbers but no analysis is like a driver with a dashboard who never learns what the warning lights mean.

### Win/loss analysis: asking the question nobody asks

After every closed deal — won or lost — ask one question: **why?** Most sellers never ask. Won deals feel like the wrong moment and lost deals feel painful, so both go unexamined, and the same pattern repeats for years.

The technique is small talk done on purpose:

- **Ask after the decision**, when emotions have settled. A phone call, not an email. People answer honestly to a voice; they write polite nothing into a form.
- **For wins, ask what almost stopped them.** "What nearly made you say no?" reveals the objections that nearly worked — your next customer will raise the same one.
- **For losses, ask about the moment it turned.** "When did you decide not to go with us?"
- **Never argue.** Thank them, take the note, hang up. You are collecting data, not reopening the deal.

Then categorise every answer into a fixed list: **price, timing, need, competitor, trust, no decision.** Track the categories month over month. One warning: the first stated reason is usually the polite reason. "We have no budget" often means "I did not trust the delivery." Dig with one follow-up — "was it the amount, or the timing of it?" — and let the pattern across many deals, not one clever answer, tell you the truth.

### Identifying the bottleneck

Deals drop out at every stage; that is the funnel. The diagnostic move is to compute the conversion rate **between each pair of stages** and find the weakest arrow:

| Stage transition | Conversion | Reading |
|------------------|-----------|---------|
| Contact → Meeting | 15% | Normal for cold |
| Meeting → Proposal | 60% | Healthy |
| Proposal → Won | 20% | **Bottleneck** |

In this example the seller is not a prospecting problem and not a presentation problem — the machine leaks at the close. The fix is closing skill, price positioning or proposal design, not more doors. A seller who guesses would probably "work harder on prospecting" and pour more water into the same hole.

Two rules:

- **Fix the weakest arrow first.** Doubling the weakest link beats improving a strong one; the strong link is not what is holding you.
- **Watch time-in-stage as well as conversion.** Deals that sit 40 days in "proposal" when the average is 12 are stalled even if they eventually close. Time is money and momentum.

### Data-driven continuous improvement

Analysis only pays if it changes behaviour. The loop is four steps, run on a fixed weekly cadence:

1. **Measure** — pull the stage numbers from your CRM.
2. **Diagnose** — name the weakest arrow and one hypothesis for why it is weak.
3. **Change one thing** — one variable: the opener, the objection handling, the proposal layout, the target list.
4. **Re-measure** — after enough volume, compare. Keep the change if it worked; revert if it did not.

Keep a **change log**: date, what you changed, what you expected, what happened. The log exists to stop the deadliest habit in selling — changing three things at once, getting a good month, and crediting the wrong one. One variable at a time is not perfectionism; it is the only way to learn anything.

### Growth hacking: the buzzer as an experiment

**Growth hacking** means cheap, fast, measured experiments to find what scales, instead of big planned campaigns. Read that definition again from a cold caller's point of view: every door you ring is a data point. The buzzer was always an experiment — most sellers just never recorded the results.

The small seller has one advantage no large competitor can buy: **iteration speed**. A corporate script change needs a committee, a training programme and a quarter. You can change your opening line tomorrow morning and know if it worked in four weeks. Treat the whole apparatus as variables: the opener, the target list, the time of day, the ask, the follow-up message. Any of them could be twice as good as what you use now, and you will never know unless you test.

### A/B testing on scripts

An **A/B test** is the clean form of the experiment: two versions, assigned at random, one measured outcome.

Worked example:

- **Version A (control):** your current opener — introduce the product.
- **Version B:** open with the problem question instead.
- **Metric:** appointment rate. Nothing else.
- **Assignment:** alternate strictly — A on the first door, B on the second, and so on.
- **Volume:** at least 100 attempts per version before you read the result.
- **Constancy:** same zone, same time of day, same list. Change only the opener.

The volume rule is the one sellers break. If your appointment rate is around 30%, a 20-call test where A got 6 and B got 8 proves nothing — that gap is ordinary luck. With 100 calls each, a 30% versus 45% split is real signal. Small tests are not "early evidence"; they are noise you have decided to believe.

One honest limit: in cold calling you rarely get a clean laboratory. Random assignment is hard when doors differ. That is fine — the goal is not a publishable study, it is a disciplined hunch with numbers behind it. Strict where you can be strict; note where you could not.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|------|---------------|--------------------|
| Win/loss analysis | Asking every closed deal why it closed the way it did | "The win/loss analysis showed trust, not price, kills our deals." |
| Bottleneck | The stage where most deals drop out | "Proposals go out but nothing closes — the bottleneck is the close." |
| Stage conversion | Percentage moving from one stage to the next | "Stage conversion from meeting to proposal is 60%." |
| Time-in-stage | How long deals sit in one stage | "Time-in-stage doubled; something is stalling proposals." |
| Change log | A record of what you changed and expected | "The change log says it was the opener, not the list." |
| Growth hacking | Cheap, fast, measured experiments to find what scales | "Every buzzer ring is a growth-hacking data point." |
| A/B test | Two versions run side by side, one metric decides | "The A/B test on the opener gave B a 15-point lift." |
| Control | The unchanged version you compare against | "Version A stayed the control all month." |
| Sample size | How many attempts stand behind a result | "Sample size was too small; the test proved nothing." |

## A field note

The sales funnel is older than any CRM. Its ancestor, the AIDA model, is credited to the American advertising pioneer E. St. Elmo Lewis; in a talk on 6 January 1910 he described the formula as "attract attention, maintain interest, arouse desire, get action," and the acronym itself first appeared in print in C. P. Russell's article "How to Write a Sales-Making Letter" in *Printers' Ink* on 2 June 1921. The funnel word followed: Arthur F. Peterson used the funnel imagery for selling in *Pharmaceutical Selling* in 1959 — because at every step of the sequence, fewer people remain. A century of knowing that buyers drop out stage by stage, and most sellers still run their pipeline from memory and mood. The tools are new; the discipline is vintage. (Source: Wikipedia, "AIDA (marketing)".)

## Exercises

1. **Four interviews.** This month, call two won customers and two lost ones. Three questions each: why us / why not us; what almost stopped you; what would you change about our process. Categorise every answer into the six buckets. Look for the pattern.
2. **Bottleneck hunt.** Pull your last 100 contacts from the CRM. Compute the conversion between each pair of stages. Name the weakest arrow and write one hypothesis for why it is weak.
3. **Design a controlled experiment.** Pick one variable — the opener is the classic. Write the protocol: version A, version B, the single metric, the assignment rule, the volume (100+ per version), what stays constant, and the date you will read the result. Run it for four weeks before judging.
4. **Start the change log.** One line per week: date, change, expected effect. At month end, write the actual effect beside each. Keep it even when the answer is "nothing changed" — that is a result too.

## Summary

- Counting tells you what happened; analysis tells you why — and only why changes next week.
- Ask every closed deal why, on the phone, and sort the answers into fixed categories; the polite reason hides the real one.
- Compute stage-to-stage conversion and fix the weakest arrow first; watch time-in-stage for stalls.
- Improve in a loop of one variable at a time, with a change log so you credit the right thing.
- The buzzer was always an experiment; an A/B test with honest sample size is what makes it science.
