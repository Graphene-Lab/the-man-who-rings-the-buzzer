# Closing for the Appointment

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Ask for the appointment plainly, in three shapes, without fear.
- Fix the next step with all five elements and confirm it the same day.
- Own the agenda and treat the calendar as the scoreboard.
- Handle "no" and "maybe" without losing the door.
- Use scheduling so that no follow-up ever slips.

## Key concepts

Everything in this book has walked toward a buzzer. The close is the moment
you simply ask — clearly, calmly — for the next meeting. There is no magic
line and no trap. Most good conversations do not die from a bad product or a
hard objection; they die of a missing next step. The deal that "went well"
and ended with no date on the calendar is the most common dead body in sales.

### Asking for the appointment

The close is a request, not a trick. Three shapes, all honest:

- **Direct ask.** "Let us put thirty minutes in with your purchasing lead.
  Does Tuesday morning work?"
- **Alternative choice.** "Tuesday at ten, or Thursday at three?" Both
  options are yes; the question is which, not whether.
- **Assumptive.** You open the calendar as you speak. The meeting is already
  real; you are only finding its slot.

Timing matters as much as shape. Plant the ask mid-meeting — "when we look at
the pilot numbers next week..." — and make the formal ask in the last ten
minutes, not the last sixty seconds. Ask more than once if the first lands
soft. And after the ask: silence. Two seconds, held (the drill from the
discovery chapter). The first person to speak after "Tuesday or Thursday?"
gives away the choice.

### Fixing the next step and confirmation

A next step is real only when it has five elements: **who** is in the room,
**what** happens, **when** it starts, **where** — the address or the link —
and **what each side brings**. "Thursday the 12th, ten o'clock, at your
office, you bring the last quarter's delivery report, I bring the pilot plan
and the ROI numbers." Recap it aloud. Watch them nod to each element.

Then confirm before you leave the building: the calendar invite goes out from
your own hand, with the recap in the description. A meeting someone else has
to schedule is a meeting that may not happen. Confirm once more the day
before: "Looking forward to ten tomorrow — I will bring the ROI numbers we
agreed." Every confirmation is a small re-close; each yes makes the final
yes larger.

### Managing the agenda and calendar

Own the agenda. Three items, time-boxed, one decision. "Twenty minutes:
five on what we found, ten on the pilot plan, five to fix the trial dates."
An owned agenda ends meetings on time and ends them with a decision; an
agenda nobody owns drifts to the loudest voice and ends with "let us think
about it."

End every meeting with the next one already named: "We will review the pilot
results on the third." The last sentence of a meeting should be the first
sentence of the next one.

Treat the calendar as the scoreboard. Count booked meetings, not
conversations. A week of warm talks with zero new dates is a losing week,
however friendly it felt. Keep a free slot each afternoon for same-day
follow-up calls — speed after a meeting is free money, and the prospect who
received your recap by four o'clock remembers who runs the process.

### Overcoming "no" and "maybe"

"No" has two kinds. Ask one question: "Is that no for this quarter, or no
for good?" A **no for now** is a parked deal: fix the return date, send the
recap, come back. A **no for good** still has value — ask for a referral and
stay warm; today's firm no is next year's easy yes, and the seller who took
it politely is the one the door remembers.

"Maybe" is a soft no wearing a friendlier face. Do not celebrate it. Shrink
the ask until saying no costs more than saying yes: "Then let us not decide
anything. Fifteen minutes on the third, I show you the pilot numbers, and
you can say no again." A small commitment reopens the path; a decision you
force closes it. What you must never do is argue with a no. Contradiction
drives the no deeper — every reframe from the objections chapter applies
here, because the close is where objections get serious.

### The closing simulation

Run it with a partner playing a busy, slightly evasive buyer. Your goal: one
booked meeting, with a confirmed date, in five minutes. The partner may only
say yes when all five elements are fixed — who, what, when, where, what each
side brings — and says "let me get back to you" to anything less. Debrief on
the ask itself: was it clear? Was it early enough? Did you hold the silence
after it, or fill it? Then swap roles: playing the buyer teaches you the
exits from the inside, and you will never again fall for one you have
performed yourself.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Close | Asking clearly for the next commitment | "The close was one sentence, not a performance." |
| Direct ask | Naming the meeting and asking for a time | "Her direct ask took eight seconds." |
| Alternative choice | Two times, both a yes | "'Tuesday ten or Thursday three' removed the no." |
| Next step | The agreed following action, fully specified | "A next step without a date is a wish." |
| Confirmation touch | A same-day or day-before re-yes | "Each confirmation touch made the final yes bigger." |
| Parked deal | A delayed deal with a fixed return date | "The parked no for Q1 reopened in April." |
| Agenda owner | The person who time-boxes and decides | "He owned the agenda and ended with a decision." |

## AgentBridge in action

The closer's worst enemy is not the buyer's "no" — it is the follow-up that
never happened. AgentBridge scheduling removes that enemy. The pattern, in
one line:

> Remind me on the 25th of every month that the supplier invoice is due.

The agent reminds you on the day, every month, delivered however you like —
chat, email or phone — and it runs even while you are away. The same one-line
pattern pins every closing commitment: the Tuesday callback, the Friday
proposal, the day-before confirmation. Deterministic Memory keeps the who,
what and when of every deal it has handled, so the reminder arrives whether
or not you remember, and the parked deal comes back to you on the third of
the month with its recap attached.

**Sales angle:** a closer with a memory that never slips never loses a deal to
a forgotten follow-up.

## A field note

The man the *Guinness Book of World Records* called the greatest salesman of
all time closed on the calendar, not on charm. Joe Girard sold 13,001 cars at
Merollis Chevrolet in Eastpointe, Michigan, between 1963 and 1978 — a
record that stood until Ali Reda sold 1,582 cars in 2017 — and was inducted
into the Automotive Hall of Fame in 2001. He later wrote a book called *How
to Close Every Sale*, but the lesson of his career was simpler than any
technique: he kept showing up, month after month, and never let a follow-up
go cold. The close, in the end, is the appointment you kept. (Source:
Wikipedia, "Joe Girard.")

## Exercises

1. **Five elements or no deal.** Role-play a busy buyer with a partner. You
   have five minutes to book a meeting; the partner says yes only when who,
   what, when, where and what-each-side-brings are all fixed. Repeat until
   you fix all five without being prompted.
2. **Ask three ways.** Write your own ask in the three shapes — direct,
   alternative choice, assumptive. Use each once in a real conversation this
   week and note which lands, with whom, and why.
3. **Park it right.** Take your last three "maybes." Write the re-entry
   message and the date for each. Send one today.
4. **Zero-slip week.** Put every follow-up commitment you make for one week
   into a reminder — paper, calendar or the agent. Miss nothing. Count at
   week's end how many deals stayed alive purely because the follow-up
   landed on time.

## Summary

- The close is a clear ask for the next meeting: plant it early, ask it
  plainly, hold the silence after.
- A next step is real only with who, what, when, where and what each side
  brings — and an invite sent the same day.
- Own the agenda; end every meeting with the next one already named, and
  count booked meetings, not conversations.
- "No for now" gets a parked date; "maybe" gets a smaller ask; arguing
  drives both deeper.
- Scheduling is the closer's safety net: a reminder that never forgets keeps
  every door open.
