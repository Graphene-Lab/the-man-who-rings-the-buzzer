# Storytelling: Telling Stories to Sell

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Explain, simply, why stories beat lists of facts for memory and trust.
- Build a sales story with situation, complication and resolution.
- Turn a customer result into a narrative case study.
- Use your own stories without sounding fake.
- Recognise when a story is the wrong tool.

## Key concepts

A fact tells someone what to think. A story lets them live it. The seller who
carries three good stories outperforms the seller with thirty good slides,
because a story does work the buyer does not have to do: it carries the argument
inside an experience. This chapter is about building that supply — and knowing
when to keep it in your bag.

### Why stories work

Research suggests three things happen in a listener's brain during a good story.
First, understanding turns into simulation: brain-imaging studies show that when
you hear "she gripped the handle," the motor areas of your own brain light up as
if you were gripping it. Second, emotion fixes memory: we remember what we felt,
and a bullet point has no feeling to hold. Third, a phenomenon called
**narrative transportation** — being carried into a story — lowers the listener's
defence. A person inside a story argues less than a person handed a claim. They
are not being sold to; they are following a character. That is why the same
argument delivered as a story lands softer and deeper than the same argument
delivered as a list.

### The story structure in sales: situation, complication, resolution

A sales story is not a ramble. It has three acts, and each act has a job.

| Act | Job | Example line |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Build a world the prospect recognises | "We worked with a roaster like you — three shops, growing fast, proud of the coffee." |
| Complication | The trouble arrives; the stakes rise | "Their packaging supplier missed two deliveries in a month. Shelves went half-empty by Friday." |
| Resolution | What was done, and the result | "We moved them to a standing order with a two-day buffer. No stockout since March." |

The middle is the point. Most sellers rush the complication to get to the happy
ending, and kill the story on the way. Spend real time in the trouble — let the
prospect feel the empty shelf — so the resolution feels earned. A story without
complication is a boast.

### Customer stories: the narrative case study

A case study in table form convinces the head. A case study told as a story
convinces the whole person. The pattern:

1. **Who.** "A client of ours, a family-owned roaster with three shops…" Name
   them only with permission; otherwise keep the type vivid.
2. **Before.** The situation, with one concrete detail.
3. **The turn.** The complication and the decision.
4. **After.** The result, with one number inside the story: "…and their Friday
   sales recovered within a month."
5. **The lesson.** One line the prospect can carry: "Standing orders don't just
   save money; they save Fridays."

Put the number inside the story, not instead of it. A bare figure is forgotten by
lunch; a figure attached to a character stays for years.

### The seller's own stories

Buyers buy people before they buy products, and they test the person constantly:
*Why do you do this? Have you actually been in the fire?* Three personal stories
carry most of that weight:

- **The origin story.** Why you do this work. Keep it short and true.
- **The failure story.** A mistake you made and what it taught you. One honest
  failure buys more trust than ten wins, because it proves you are not selling
  a mask.
- **The moment-of-truth story.** A time the stakes were real and you chose the
  customer's side, even when it cost you.

Authenticity is the whole price of admission. Do not embellish. An invented story
that unravels in due diligence costs you the deal and your name; a true story
told plainly costs nothing and works better anyway.

### When NOT to use storytelling

Stories are a tool, not a personality. Put the tool down when:

- **They asked for a number.** "What does it cost per unit?" wants the number
  first. Answer directly; the story can follow if it adds something.
- **Price and contract talks.** A story around a price question feels evasive.
  State the price, hold the silence.
- **The buyer thinks in tables.** Some engineers and finance people want data
  before narrative. Give the data; offer the story after.
- **The story is about you, not them.** If the moral of your story is "I am
  great," they hear it. Retell it or drop it.
- **Their pain is raw.** Do not dramatise a wound someone is still bleeding
  from. Solve, don't perform.

### Your story bank

Do not invent stories at the door. Carry them. Build a **story bank** of eight to
twelve entries: three customer wins, two failures with lessons, two origin or
"why I do this" stories, two competitor contrasts, one personal moment of
truth. Tag each story with the objection it answers — "too expensive," "we've
never had problems," "why change now?" — so when the objection arrives you pull
the matching story instead of improvising. A story bank is rehearsed, never
memorised: the shape is fixed, the words are fresh.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative transportation | Being carried into a story so you stop arguing | "The case study put him inside the story; transportation did the rest." |
| Story bank | A prepared set of tagged stories | "Her story bank had an answer for every objection in the meeting." |
| Complication | The trouble in the middle of the story | "He spent a full minute on the complication before the fix." |
| Narrative case study | A customer result told as a story | "We turned the quarterly report into a narrative case study." |
| Authenticity | Being real; no embellishment | "The failure story worked because it was authentic." |

## A field note

Why do stories move people to act? In 2015 the neuroeconomist Paul Zak and
colleagues published a study in *Biological Psychology* ("The heart of the
story") in which participants watched short, character-driven films while their
physiology was measured. The stronger their empathy-linked physical response
during the story, the more they later donated to charity. Character plus
emotional tension, it turns out, is a mechanism for moving people — which is
exactly why the complication in your sales story is not decoration. It is the
engine. (Source: Wikipedia, "Paul Zak," citing Barraza, Nazarian & Zak, 2015.)

## Exercises

1. **Three acts.** Write one customer story in situation–complication–resolution.
   Read it aloud; it should run about ninety seconds.
2. **Complication airtime.** Record yourself telling it. If the complication
   takes less than a third of the time, rewrite. The story lives or dies in the
   middle.
3. **Build the bank.** List ten stories: three wins, two failures with lessons,
   two origin, two contrasts, one personal. Tag each with the objection it
   answers.
4. **The "so what?" test.** Tell a story to a partner. They ask "so what?"
   Answer with the lesson in one line. If you cannot, the story is not ready.

## Summary

- Stories work because the listener simulates them, feels them, and stops
  arguing while inside them.
- Three acts: situation, complication, resolution — and the complication is
  the engine.
- A narrative case study puts one number inside one character's arc.
- Your own stories, especially one honest failure, buy trust no slide can.
- Know when to drop the story: direct questions, price talks, raw pain.
