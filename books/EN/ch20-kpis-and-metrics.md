# KPIs and Metrics of Cold Selling

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Name the core KPIs of cold B2B selling and compute each one from your own activity.
- Build your personal funnel and read which stage is weak.
- Keep a CRM that produces honest data through light daily discipline.
- Run a weekly pipeline review and produce a simple weighted forecast.
- Build your own one-page KPI dashboard.

## Key concepts

### If you cannot count it, you cannot improve it

Cold selling looks like an art: charisma, timing, courage at the buzzer. Underneath, it is arithmetic. Every cold seller runs the same machine:

> activity → contacts → conversations → appointments → proposals → deals

Each arrow is a number. A **KPI** (Key Performance Indicator) is simply a number you watch because it tells you whether you are moving toward your goal. If you know your numbers, you know which arrow is weak and where to put effort. If you do not, you are guessing — and you will feel like a genius in lucky weeks and a failure in unlucky ones, when both weeks are just variance.

### The core KPIs

| KPI | Plain definition | How to compute |
|-----|------------------|----------------|
| Activity volume | Doors you knock, calls you make, emails you send | Count per day |
| Contact rate | Share of attempts that reach a human | Contacts ÷ attempts |
| Appointment rate | Share of real conversations that end in a booked meeting | Appointments ÷ conversations |
| Show rate | Share of booked meetings that actually happen | Meetings held ÷ meetings booked |
| Conversion rate | Share of opportunities that become paying deals | Deals won ÷ opportunities |
| Cycle length | Days from first contact to signed deal | Average across won deals |
| Average deal value | What one customer is worth | Total revenue ÷ number of deals |
| Cost per appointment | What one meeting costs you | Total cost ÷ appointments |

Benchmarks vary widely by industry, ticket size and geography, so treat published averages with suspicion. Your own history is the only honest comparison: this month against last month, you against you.

### The funnel math: where the levers are

Say you ring 100 buzzers in a week. Forty people let you in or answer. Twenty become real conversations. Six end with a booked appointment. Three proposals go out. One deal closes.

Your cold conversion is 1 deal per 100 buzzers. Now look at the arrows:

- Contact rate: 40%
- Conversation-to-appointment: 30%
- Appointment-to-proposal: 50%
- Proposal-to-close: 33%

Low cold conversion is normal — do not judge yourself against warm-lead close rates. The point of the numbers is **lever logic**: if you double your appointment rate, your deals double without ringing one extra door. If you double activity instead, you double your effort. Counting tells you which lever is cheapest to pull.

### CRM and data entry: the honest diary

A **CRM** (Customer Relationship Management system) is a database of every company you contact, every conversation you have, and every deal you open. It is the instrument panel for the machine above.

One rule governs everything: **garbage in, garbage out.** A CRM filled late, vaguely, or not at all produces numbers you cannot trust, and untrustworthy numbers are worse than none — they make bad decisions feel justified. Four habits keep the data honest:

- **Log the same day.** Memory dies by evening. A two-line note at the gate beats a novel tomorrow.
- **One company, one record.** Duplicates hide real history.
- **Fixed outcome codes.** Every attempt gets one: no answer, gatekeeper blocked, not interested, callback agreed, meeting booked. Free text is where data goes to die.
- **Never leave the stage blank.** Every open deal sits in a named stage.

Data entry is not paperwork for the office. It is your evidence base. If you did not log it, you cannot learn from it — and you cannot forecast from it either.

### Conversion and pipeline analysis: measure the arrows, not the ends

Your **pipeline** is the list of all open deals, lined up by stage. Most sellers look only at the end: what closed. That number tells you whether you are fed or starving, but not why. The stage-to-stage conversion rates tell you where the machine leaks. A 15% contact-to-meeting rate with a 20% proposal-to-close rate is a different disease than the reverse, and it needs a different cure. We diagnose this properly in the chapter on pipeline analysis; here, just start measuring every arrow.

### Forecast and pipeline review

A **forecast** is your estimate of what will close in the coming period. The simplest honest method is the **weighted pipeline**: multiply each open deal by the historical probability of its stage. A €10,000 deal sitting at "proposal" — a stage that historically closes 30% of the time — contributes €3,000 to the forecast. Add the weighted values and you have a number that is neither hope nor pessimism, but history applied to today's deals.

Then hold a **pipeline review**: the same half hour, the same day, every week. Five questions:

1. What moved this week, and why?
2. What is stuck, and what is stuck about it?
3. What will close this period?
4. Is the top of the funnel big enough for the next period?
5. What will I do differently this week?

Track your **forecast accuracy** as a KPI of its own: forecast versus actual, every period. A forecast you never check is a wish.

### Dashboards and reports

A **dashboard** is one page with the few numbers you look at weekly. Keep it small — six to ten figures. Trends beat snapshots: a number without last month's number beside it is trivia. Review the dashboard every week, before you plan the week, not after. The seller who checks the dashboard first plans from evidence; the seller who checks it last writes a story around whatever happened.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|------|---------------|--------------------|
| KPI | A number that shows whether you are hitting your goal | "My top KPI is appointments per week." |
| Conversion rate | Share of one thing that becomes the next | "My proposal-to-close conversion is 33%." |
| Appointment rate | Share of conversations that end in a meeting | "The new opener lifted my appointment rate to 30%." |
| Cycle length | Days from first contact to signed deal | "Our cycle length is 45 days, so this month's doors feed next quarter." |
| Pipeline | All your open deals, ordered by stage | "The pipeline is thin; I need more buzzers this week." |
| Weighted pipeline | Each deal multiplied by its stage's win probability | "The weighted pipeline says €24,000 closes this month." |
| Forecast accuracy | Forecast versus what actually happened | "My forecast accuracy improved once I stopped hoping." |
| Data hygiene | How clean and complete your records are | "Bad data hygiene made the whole report useless." |

## A field note

In a lecture on "Electrical Units of Measurement" delivered on 3 May 1883, the physicist William Thomson — Lord Kelvin — said: "I often say that when you can measure what you are speaking about, and express it in numbers, you know something about it; but when you cannot measure it, when you cannot express it in numbers, your knowledge is of a meagre and unsatisfactory kind." Kelvin was talking about physics, but he described the sales dashboard a century early. Every technique in this book — the opener, the buzzer approach, the follow-up — is "the beginning of knowledge" until you count what it produces. Measurement is what turns a craftsperson's hunch into a repeatable machine. (Source: Wikiquote, William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin; lecture published in *Popular Lectures*, Vol. I.)

## Exercises

1. **Compute your funnel.** Take last month's real activity: attempts, contacts, conversations, appointments, proposals, deals. Write the five stage-to-stage conversion rates. Name the weakest arrow.
2. **Build your dashboard.** Use a spreadsheet or AgentBridge. Six to ten numbers: activity, appointment rate, proposals out, deals won, revenue, pipeline value, forecast. Put a chart of the revenue trend on it. Look at it every Monday before you plan.
3. **Forecast and check.** Forecast next period with the weighted-pipeline method. At period end, compare forecast to actual and record the gap. Repeat monthly and watch your accuracy tighten.
4. **Hygiene audit.** Open your CRM right now. Count records with a blank stage, blank outcome code, or no note in 14 days. That count is your debt. Clear it this week, then keep it under five.

## Summary

- Cold selling is arithmetic: activity → contacts → conversations → appointments → proposals → deals, and every arrow is a number.
- The core KPIs are contact rate, appointment rate, conversion rate, cycle length, average deal value and cost per appointment.
- The CRM is your evidence base; log same-day, use fixed outcome codes, never leave a stage blank.
- Forecast with the weighted pipeline and track forecast accuracy — an unchecked forecast is a wish.
- Keep one small dashboard and review it weekly before you plan, not after.
