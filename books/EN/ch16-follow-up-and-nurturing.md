# Follow-Up and Nurturing

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Design a **cadence** (a planned sequence of timed contacts) for a live deal.
- Pick the right **channel** — email, phone, Telegram, in person — for each step.
- Send content that is useful rather than needy, so the prospect keeps reading.
- Personalise at scale and automate the timing without losing the human touch.
- Avoid the follow-up mistakes that quietly kill good deals.

## Key concepts

### The buzzer does not stop at the door

Ringing the buzzer gets you heard once. Most sales are not won at the first ring. They are won by the person who stays present, in a welcome way, until the buyer is ready. Follow-up is the courage to return. Nurturing is the skill of making each return worth the prospect's time. A pushy follow-up is a stranger banging on the door again. A good follow-up is a helpful neighbour who brings something useful each time.

### Cadence: the timed sequence

A **cadence** is the order and timing of your contacts on one deal. Do not improvise it. Write it down before you send the quote. A simple cadence for a small B2B deal looks like this:

| Step | When | Channel | Purpose |
|------|------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Day 0 | Email | Send the quote, confirm what you understood |
| 2 | Day 2 | Phone or email | Check it arrived and made sense |
| 3 | Day 7 | Email | Add value: a case, a number, an answer to their question |
| 4 | Day 14 | Phone | Handle the real objection, ask for the decision |
| 5 | Day 21 | Email | A polite break-up that leaves the door open |

The exact days matter less than the shape: contact, add value, ask, and never vanish. Most deals that "died" did not die from a "no". They died from silence on your side.

### Channels: match the message to the medium

Each channel does one job well. Use it for that job.

- **Email** is your paper trail. Use it for the quote, the summary, the formal nudge. It is searchable and low-pressure.
- **Phone** is for warmth and for the hard question. A tone of voice settles doubt that text cannot.
- **Telegram or chat** is for the quick, friendly ping — a link, a one-line check-in, a file on the move.
- **In person** is for the close and for repair. When something is stuck, show up.

A common mistake is doing everything by email. Text cannot hear hesitation. Move to the phone when you sense a "but".

### Useful content: never "just checking in"

The fastest way to be ignored is to add nothing. Every touch must give the reader something: a short case study of a customer like them, one number that matters to their business, an article that answers a question they raised, or a clear answer to a doubt. The rule is simple: **each contact should be worth opening even if they were not going to buy.** When your follow-ups are useful, you stop being a pest and start being a source.

### Personalisation and automation

Personalisation and automation are not enemies. Automate the **timing**; keep the **words** human. A scheduled reminder that pings you on day 7 is automation. The line that opens with the prospect's own words from your last call is personalisation. Do the first sentence by hand every time — reference something specific they said. Let a tool handle the "don't forget" part. What you must never automate is a generic message sent unchanged to a hundred people. That reads as spam and is treated as spam.

### Mistakes to avoid

- **No value.** "Just checking in" asks for attention and offers nothing.
- **Quitting too soon.** One or two tries is not a follow-up. Most yes answers come after the third contact.
- **Over-messaging.** Ten pings in a week is harassment, not persistence. Space your cadence.
- **No next step.** End every touch with one clear, easy ask: a call time, a yes/no, a question.
- **One message for everyone.** If it could go to anyone, it will move no one.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|------|---------------|--------------------|
| Cadence | The planned timing and order of your contacts | "Your cadence is too loose; add a call on day 7." |
| Nurture | To keep a relationship warm until it is ready | "We nurture cold leads with a monthly newsletter." |
| Touchpoint | One single contact in the sequence | "The third touchpoint is where most replies arrive." |
| Break-up email | A final, polite message that closes the loop and leaves the door open | "The break-up email often gets the reply the others didn't." |
| Automation | A tool that runs the timing for you | "Automation sends the reminder; you write the line." |
| Personalisation | Tailoring a message to one person | "Personalise the first line with what they told you." |

## AgentBridge in action

A client has gone quiet on your quote. You do not want to nag, but you do not want to vanish either. You type:

> **Write a short follow-up to a client who has not replied to our quote from last week.**

AgentBridge writes a light, polite nudge that reminds without pressure — a real draft you can read once and send. And because the agent runs scheduled tasks, you can add one line: *"if they have not replied by Friday, remind me to call."* The timing is now handled; the warmth is still yours.

**Sales angle:** the deal stays alive because *you* stay alive in it. The follow-up that used to slip through the cracks now arrives on time, in your voice, every time.

## A field note

Joe Girard, a Chevrolet salesman in Michigan, held the Guinness World Record for the most cars sold by one person in a single year — 1,425 in 1973 — and sold 13,001 cars across fifteen years (1963–1978) at Merollis Chevrolet before retiring at 49. The record was not one lucky sale; it was fifteen straight years of staying in front of the same market. As he put it in his book *How to Sell Anything to Anybody* (2006), the business is not the first sale, it is the relationship after it. (Source: Wikipedia, "Joe Girard"; Guinness World Records.)

## Exercises

1. **Build a cadence.** Take the last quote that stalled. Write a five-step cadence for it in the table format above: day, channel, purpose. Give it a break-up step.
2. **Three useful nudges.** Write three follow-up messages for that same deal that each add value — a case, a number, an answer. Ban the phrase "just checking in."
3. **Set the timing.** On your phone or with a tool, schedule the day-2 and day-7 reminders for that deal right now. Notice how the weight leaves your head when the timing is handled.

## Summary

- Follow-up is showing up again; nurturing is making each showing-up worth the prospect's time.
- A written cadence beats improvisation: contact, add value, ask, never vanish.
- Match the channel to the message — email for the trail, phone for the doubt, chat for the quick ping.
- Never send "just checking in"; every touch must be worth opening.
- Automate the timing, keep the words human, and personalise the first line every time.
