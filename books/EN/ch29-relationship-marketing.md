# Relationship Marketing: The Buzzer as the First Act of a Relationship

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Explain why a B2B sale is the opening of a relationship, not a single event.
- Reframe the first buzzer as sowing, not reaping.
- Map the touchpoints that turn one cold visit into a running account.
- Use memory and follow-through so a client feels known, not worked.
- Tell the difference between a contact list and a real relationship.

## Key concepts

### The science: bonds, not transactions

For years, sales training treated a deal as a line on a spreadsheet. Relationship
marketing starts from a different fact: in business-to-business selling, people buy
from people they trust, and trust is built by repeated, reliable contact over time.
No one hands you a large order on the first meeting. They hand it to you on the
seventh, because of what happened on the first six.

This is not soft. Buying in a company is a social act. The person you spoke to must
defend the choice to a boss, a colleague, a finance lead. Every warm, consistent
interaction you give them becomes a reason they can repeat to others: "they know
us, they follow through, they are safe." Recall plus consistency is the raw
material of trust. A single clever pitch is forgotten. A pattern of small, kept
promises is not.

Think of the buzzer this way. You are not trying to close a door-to-door sale before
the gate closes. You are starting a series. The value of the visit is measured in
what comes after it, not in what you sign on the step.

### The first visit should sow, not reap

Most door-to-door sellers fail at the buzzer for one reason: they try to harvest on
the first visit. They push for the order, the signature, the yes. The prospect feels
the pressure and shuts the gate.

A relationship seller does the opposite. The first buzzer is for sowing. You plant
three seeds and leave:

1. **A useful thing.** A fact, a small idea, a sample, a number that helps them,
   given freely with no strings. You proved you can add value before you ask for any.
2. **A reason to remember you.** One clear line about who you are and the single
   problem you solve. Not your whole catalogue. One thing.
3. **A next step.** A concrete, easy reason to meet again: "Let me bring you a
   short comparison of what this would cost you. Tuesday?"

You rarely close on a sowing visit. That is fine. A seed is not a harvest. The
seller who understands this does not feel like they wasted the trip; they know they
planted it.

### Tactical application: building the bond

A relationship is not a feeling you wait for. It is a set of actions you repeat.
Around every account, build these habits:

- **Follow through on every promise, small or large.** If you say "I will send the
  pricing," send it the same day. A broken small promise costs more than a missed
  big one, because it is the one they expected you to keep.
- **Remember the personal details.** The child's exam, the factory move, the
  supplier that keeps them awake. Write them down. Bring them up next time. Being
  remembered is one of the strongest bonds there is.
- **Give between the asks.** Do not only appear when you want an order. Send a
  useful article, a market note, a heads-up about a change that affects them. Value
  with no invoice attached.
- **Be the easy one to work with.** Answer quickly. Make the next step obvious.
  Remove friction. People stay with the vendor who makes their life simpler.
- **Keep the rhythm.** A relationship dies in silence. Agree a cadence — a monthly
  note, a quarterly visit — and keep it even when nothing is for sale.

### Continuity is the moat

Products get copied. Prices get undercut. What is almost impossible to copy is the
sense that you have known this client for years, that you remember everything, that
starting from scratch with someone else would mean teaching a stranger from zero.
That continuity is your moat. The competitor can match your price. They cannot
match your shared history.

This is where memory stops being a personal talent and becomes a system. The
problem for any seller with a growing book of clients is that human memory leaks.
You forget the small detail, the last quote, the exact promise. The vendor who
never forgets has a structural advantage. Keep this in mind for the tool box at the
end of the chapter.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | What it means, simply | Used in a real sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship marketing | Selling by building long-term trust instead of chasing one-off deals | "We moved to relationship marketing and stopped chasing orders; the orders started coming back on their own." |
| Touchpoint | Any moment a client comes into contact with you | "The buzzer is touchpoint one; the follow-up email is touchpoint two." |
| Sowing vs reaping | Giving value first (sowing) before asking for the sale (reaping) | "The first visit is sowing; you reap on the third or fourth." |
| Customer lifetime value | The total a client is worth across all the years, not one order | "One invoice is small; the lifetime value of keeping them for five years is the real prize." |
| Continuity | The unbroken thread of shared history with an account | "Continuity is why they will not leave for a cheaper vendor who knows nothing about them." |
| Recall | The client's ability to remember you and what you said | "Warm recall from a good first visit is worth more than a perfect pitch they forget by lunch." |

## A field note

The term "relationship marketing" was first proposed by the American marketing
scholar Leonard L. Berry in his 1983 paper "Relationship Marketing," published in
*Emerging Perspectives on Services Marketing* (American Marketing Association).
Barbara B. Jackson carried it into industrial and business-to-business selling in
1985. The idea was radical at the time: instead of treating each sale as a separate
win, firms should invest in keeping and growing existing customers, because a loyal
account is worth far more than a string of one-off transactions. Nearly every
modern "customer success" and account-management practice traces back to that shift.

## Exercises

1. **Map your touchpoints.** Pick one real account. On paper, list every contact
   you have had with them in the last six months — visits, calls, emails, messages.
   Count them. If the number is under five, you have a contact, not a relationship.
   Write down three touchpoints you could add this month that give value with no ask.
2. **Rewrite your first visit.** Take your usual buzzer pitch. Cut every line that
   asks for the order. Replace it with one useful thing you can give for free, one
   clear line about who you are, and one concrete next step with a day attached.
   Practise it out loud.
3. **The promise audit.** List every small promise you have made to clients this
   month ("I'll send that," "I'll check"). Mark which you kept. For each broken
   one, send a fix today. Notice how many you find.
4. **Build a memory habit.** For your top three accounts, write down three personal
   or business details you would hate to forget before the next visit. Store them
   somewhere you will actually retrieve them — a note, a file, or your agent's
   memory — and review them before you ring.

## Summary

- In B2B, trust is built by repeated, reliable contact; the sale is the start of a
  series, not a single event.
- The first buzzer is for sowing: give value, be memorable, set a next step. Do
  not try to harvest on visit one.
- A relationship is a set of repeated actions — follow-through, remembering,
  giving between asks, keeping the rhythm.
- Continuity is your moat: a competitor can match your price, not your shared
  history.
- Memory is the bottleneck of relationship selling; a system that never forgets
  turns a personal talent into a structural advantage.
