# Discovering Needs: Questions and Listening

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Practise active listening: hear the content and the feeling, and show it.
- Use strategic silence without flinching.
- Choose open, closed and investigative questions on purpose.
- Run a SPIN conversation and find the gap the sale lives in.
- Qualify with BANT or CHAMP without sounding like an audit.

## Key concepts

The pitch is your opening. Discovery is the meeting. Amateurs think selling means
talking well; professionals know it means asking well and hearing what comes
back. The buzzer got you through the door. Questions and listening decide what
you are actually selling — and whether the customer already knows they need it.

### Active listening

**Active listening** means listening to understand, not to reply. Most people
spend a conversation composing their next sentence. The active listener does
three things instead:

- **Reflect.** Feed back the feeling: "That delay clearly cost you a good
  customer."
- **Summarise.** Compress the facts: "So, if I have this right: two late
  deliveries, one lost promotion, and purchasing is looking at alternatives."
- **Confirm.** Check before moving: "Is that all, or is there more?"

The confirm question is the most neglected and the most valuable. "Is there
more?" often produces the real issue — the one they were holding back.

### Strategic silence

After the prospect stops talking, count two seconds before you speak. Do not
answer your own question. Do not fill the pause. **Strategic silence** feels
awkward for exactly the person who is breaking it — and it is almost always the
prospect. People fill silence with the thing they were about to leave out: the
budget worry, the internal politics, the real deadline. The seller who can hold
two quiet seconds learns more than the one who asks a clever follow-up.

### Types of questions

| Type | Shape | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open | What / How / Why… | Opens a wide area; cannot be answered yes/no | "How do you handle returns today?" |
| Closed | Yes / No | Confirms a fact; pins a decision | "Is that under your budget?" |
| Investigative | A drill into one answer | Goes deep on what they just said | "You said returns pile up — how does that hit the warehouse?" |

Work as a funnel: open to find the territory, investigative to dig, closed to
confirm. Do not live in closed questions — you get facts but no story. Do not
drift in open ones — you get a story but no commitment. The investigative
question is where discovery actually happens, because it takes one word the
prospect gave you and turns it into a vein: "You mentioned *frustrating* — what
does that look like on a Monday morning?"

### The SPIN method and gap selling

**SPIN** is the question sequence developed from the largest research program
ever run on selling behaviour (see the field note below). Four kinds of question,
in order:

| Letter | Question type | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | Situation | Catch what your research missed — few and careful | "Who handles your packaging orders today?" |
| P | Problem | Surface the pain | "How often do deliveries arrive late?" |
| I | Implication | Make the pain cost something out loud | "What does a late delivery cost you in lost sales?" |
| N | Need-payoff | Let them voice the value of the fix | "If deliveries never missed, what would that free up for you?" |

The implication question is the one most sellers skip, and the one that changes
the deal. A small problem stated plainly stays small; the same problem followed
through its consequences — lost promotions, angry staff, a search for new
suppliers — becomes urgent. The need-payoff question then lets the prospect sell
themselves: people believe the solution they described.

**Gap selling** is the same idea in one picture. Every sale is a gap between
where the customer is now and where they want to be. Your questions map the
current state, sketch the desired state, and widen the honest distance between
them. The wider the true gap, the bigger the sale. The warning is in the word
*honest*: find real gaps, never invent them. A manufactured gap collapses the
first time the customer checks.

### BANT and CHAMP

Qualification is deciding whether this door is worth the floor. Two classic
frameworks:

| BANT | CHAMP |
|---|---|
| Budget — can they pay? | Challenges — what hurts? |
| Authority — can they decide? | Authority — who decides? |
| Need — do they need it? | Money — what can they pay? |
| Timeline — when? | Prioritisation — how soon vs everything else? |

BANT checks from the seller's side first: money, then power, then need. Used
blind, it feels like an audit and prospects close up. CHAMP starts with the
customer's challenge, so the qualification feels like help, and budget comes
only after the problem is real. Use CHAMP when the relationship is new and trust
is thin. Use BANT when they have already agreed the problem and you need to
check the practicalities before investing. Both are maps, not interrogations —
the questions should still be open, still curious, still conversational.

### Role play

Discovery is a skill of the moment, and the moment cannot be read into you.
Practise with a partner. One plays a prospect with a real business problem; the
other gets five minutes and may ask questions only — no statements, no pitching.
Then debrief with counts: how many open, how many closed, how many investigative?
Did any silence get held, or did the questioner rush? Did they find a gap, or
stay on the surface? Repeat until the question-only discipline feels natural. It
will feel unnatural right up until the moment it saves a deal.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Active listening | Hearing content and feeling, and showing it | "Her active listening turned a complaint into a case study." |
| Strategic silence | A deliberate pause that invites more | "He held two seconds and she filled them with the budget truth." |
| Open question | Wide; cannot be answered yes/no | "An open question opened the whole returns mess." |
| Investigative question | A drill into one answer | "His investigative question caught the word 'frustrating' and dug." |
| SPIN | Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff questions | "The implication question made the small delay feel expensive." |
| Gap | The distance between now and wanted | "The sale lives in the gap." |
| BANT / CHAMP | Qualification frameworks | "We switched to CHAMP because BANT felt like an audit." |

## AgentBridge in action

Every fact your agent finds is a question you do not waste. Discovery is better
when the boring facts are already known, so your questions go to the gap instead
of the brochure. Before the meeting:

> Look up our three main competitors and summarise their prices and what they
> highlight.

The agent browses the competitors' pages and brings back a clear comparison of
prices and positioning, with the links so you can check. And from your own
files:

> What is our cancellation policy with the printing supplier?

The agent searches your documents area and answers with what your own files
actually say, pointing to the source. Walk in knowing the market, your own
terms, and the likely objections. Now "How are you handling this today?" is not
a fishing question — it is a professional's opening, and every answer lands on
ground you have already prepared.

**Sales angle:** discovery is a scarce resource — thirty minutes and a
stranger's patience. AgentBridge spends none of it on facts you could have
learned at your desk, so every question you ask earns its place.

## A field note

SPIN selling was not invented at a whiteboard. Neil Rackham and a team of thirty
researchers spent twelve years and roughly one million dollars — funded by
companies including Xerox and IBM — studying more than 35,000 real sales calls
across over twenty countries. It was the first large scientific study of selling
and buying behaviour, and its finding was uncomfortable: in large sales, the
clever closing tricks of the traditional sales course did not predict success.
What predicted success was the questions — the Situation, Problem, Implication
and Need-payoff sequence he published in *SPIN Selling* in 1988. Rejected by
several publishers at first, it became a New York Times business bestseller, and
Inc. magazine later ranked it the number-one how-to-sell book of all time.
(Source: Wikipedia, "Neil Rackham.")

## Exercises

1. **Questions only.** With a partner, run five minutes where you may ask only
   questions — no statements, no pitch. Debrief with counts by type.
2. **The two-second drill.** In your next three real conversations, wait two
   full seconds after every answer before you speak. Note what the silence
   produced.
3. **One of each.** Write one SPIN question per letter for your own product.
   Test the implication question on a colleague: does the problem get more
   expensive as they answer?
4. **Map the needs.** Pick a real prospect. On one page write their current
   state, their desired state, and the gap between. Then write the three
   questions that would widen that gap honestly — and use them at your next
   meeting.

## Summary

- Discovery is the meeting: ask well, hear what comes back, and let the
  prospect sell themselves.
- Active listening plus two seconds of silence surfaces what the questions
  alone would miss.
- Funnel the question types: open to find, investigative to dig, closed to
  confirm.
- SPIN and gap selling turn a small stated problem into an urgent one —
  honestly, never by invention.
- AgentBridge pre-loads the facts from the web and your own documents so your
  questions are spent on the gap, not the brochure.
