# Ethics, Privacy, and Reputation

## Objectives

After this chapter you can:

- Handle prospect data in a way that respects GDPR and basic decency.
- Write and follow a personal code of conduct at the buzzer.
- Treat reputation as a compounding asset, not a marketing slogan.
- Work the hard edge cases — data you should not collect, claims you should not make.
- Build a personal ethical code you can state in one breath.

## Key concepts

Sales has a long, unflattering history: the pushy vendor, the hidden catch, the
promise made to close and broken after. That history is the tax every honest
seller pays at the door — the prospect starts distrustful because the profession
earned it. Ethics is not a constraint that slows you down. It is the only thing
that makes the next sale possible. In a market where one bad reputation travels
faster than any campaign, how you handle data, promises, and people *is* your
strategy.

### GDPR and data handling

GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — is the European Union's rulebook
for handling personal data. It applies to anyone who processes information about
people in the EU, including a seller with a notebook of prospects. The core
duties, in plain words:

- **Lawful basis.** Have a real reason to hold someone's personal data, and be
  able to name it.
- **Purpose limitation.** Collect data for a stated reason and do not quietly
  reuse it for something else.
- **Data minimisation.** Hold only what you actually need. A name and a business
  email is often enough; you do not need a home address.
- **Storage limitation.** Do not keep it forever. Delete what has gone stale.
- **Rights of the person.** People can ask what you hold, correct it, and have
  it erased. You must be able to honour that.

For the man at the buzzer this is practical, not legal theory. Keep your prospect
data tidy and minimal. Say why you are collecting it. When someone says "remove
me," remove them, today. Do not buy lists of scraped personal data and spray them
— that is where sellers get fined and lose their standing at once.

### A code of conduct

A code of conduct is a short list of promises you make to yourself so you do not
have to decide ethics under pressure, in the moment, when a commission is on the
line. Write it down. Keep it to a handful of lines you can recite. A working
seller's code:

- Never lie about what the product does, even by omission.
- Never promise a date or a result you are not sure you can deliver.
- Never take data you do not need or did not tell them you were taking.
- Never talk down a competitor with a claim you cannot prove.
- Never pressure someone into a decision they will regret.
- When you make a mistake, say so first, and fix it at your cost.

The point of a code is consistency. A buyer who learns you keep your word on the
small things trusts you on the big things. A code you only follow when it is
convenient is not a code; it is a mood.

### Reputation and sustainability

Reputation is the interest you earn on every promise you keep, and the interest
you pay on every one you break. It compounds. A seller who keeps their word for
five years has an asset no advertising budget can buy: people answer the buzzer
because they already know the name is good. A seller who cheats once has a
liability that follows them — and in a small professional world, everyone knows
everyone.

This is why ethics and long-term profit point the same way. The cheat optimises one
deal and destroys the next hundred. The honest seller optimises the relationship
and lets the referrals compound. If you plan to sell for a decade, honesty is not
virtue; it is arithmetic. Reputation is the only sustainable growth engine in a
business built on trust.

### Edge cases

The easy cases are obvious. The hard ones are where a code earns its keep.

- **The data you were given freely.** A prospect hands you their card and says
  "keep me in mind." That is consent for this purpose, not a licence to add them
  to every list you own. Use it as given.
- **The truth that hurts the sale.** You learn your product is a poor fit for
  this buyer. The short-term move is to hide it. The right move is to say so —
  and you keep the relationship and the referral.
- **The competitor's secret.** You learn something private about a rival. You
  did not earn the right to use it. Do not.
- **The exaggerated number.** "Roughly doubles your results" when you mean
  "maybe ten per cent." Round honestly or do not round at all.
- **The personal favour.** A contact shares confidential information to help
  you. Honour the confidence even when it costs the deal.

In every edge case the test is the same: would I be comfortable if this were
written down and shown to the person it is about? If not, do not do it.

## Terminology in practice

| Term | Plain meaning | Used in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Personal data | Any information about an identifiable person | "A prospect's work email is personal data under GDPR." |
| Lawful basis | The legal reason you may hold data | "Their enquiry is a lawful basis to keep the contact." |
| Data minimisation | Hold only what you need | "Data minimisation: a business email, not a home address." |
| Right to erasure | A person can ask you to delete their data | "Honour the right to erasure the same day." |
| Code of conduct | Promises you make to yourself | "His code of conduct: never promise what he can't deliver." |
| Reputation compounding | Trust that grows on itself over time | "Her reputation compounding brought three referrals a month." |

## A field note

GDPR did not appear from nowhere. It replaced the EU's earlier Data Protection
Directive, 95/46/EC, which dated from 1995 — the dawn of the commercial web. The
General Data Protection Regulation was adopted by the Council of the European
Union on 8 April 2016 and by the European Parliament on 14 April 2016, and
became directly applicable on 25 May 2018. Its teeth are real: the maximum fine
is 20 million euros or 4 per cent of a company's annual worldwide turnover for
the preceding year, whichever is greater. For a seller the lesson is concrete:
data handling stopped being a courtesy and became a regulated duty with a price
tag. (Source: Wikipedia, "General Data Protection Regulation.")

## Exercises

1. **Data audit.** Open your real prospect list. For each field, ask: do I need
   this, and do I have a lawful basis to hold it? Delete what fails. Write one
   line you would say to a prospect about why you hold their data.
2. **Write your code.** Draft a personal code of conduct in six lines or fewer,
   each a promise you can keep under pressure. Read it aloud. Memorise it.
3. **The edge-case drill.** Take the five edge cases above. For each, write the
   sentence you would actually say to do the right thing out loud. Practise
   until the honest line comes as fast as the easy lie.
4. **The disclosure test.** Pick your three best sales claims. For each, ask:
   would I be comfortable if this exact claim were shown to the customer and to
   a regulator? Downgrade any claim that fails the test to the honest number.

- In every edge case, apply the disclosure test: would I show this to the person
